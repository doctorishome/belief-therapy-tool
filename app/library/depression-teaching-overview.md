1. What it is

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a mood disorder. It involves a lasting change from a person's usual self, marked by persistently low mood and/or a loss of interest or pleasure, along with changes in body, thinking, and behavior. It is episodic: a person can have one episode, but recurrence is common. It causes real distress or impairment in work, school, relationships, or self-care. It is not ordinary sadness, and it is not normal grief, although grief can turn into depression. It is also not explained better by a substance, a medical condition, or a bipolar-spectrum disorder.

Counselor framing: depression is common, treatable, and often recurrent. Assessment always includes screening for bipolar history (any past mania or hypomania), substance use, medical contributors (for example, thyroid disease or medication effects), and safety.

2. Core symptoms to watch for

The DSM-5-TR criteria are summarized here in plain language. Use the manual itself for formal diagnosis.

Duration: symptoms are present most of the day, nearly every day, for at least two weeks.

Count: at least five symptoms from the list below. At least one of the five must be low mood or loss of interest/pleasure.

1.Depressed, sad, empty, or hopeless mood. In children and teens this may show up as irritability.

2.Clearly reduced interest or pleasure in most activities (anhedonia).

3.Significant change in appetite or weight, up or down.

4.Sleeping too little or too much.

5.Visible slowing down, or visible restlessness and agitation, that others can observe.

6.Fatigue or loss of energy.

7.Feelings of worthlessness or excessive or inappropriate guilt.

8.Trouble concentrating, thinking, or making decisions.

9.Recurrent thoughts of death, suicidal thoughts, a plan, or an attempt.

Related presentations: Persistent depressive disorder means chronic depressed mood lasting two years or more in adults (one year in youth). Specifiers such as anxious distress, melancholic features, psychotic features, peripartum onset, and seasonal pattern shape treatment planning.

Suicide risk note (general clinical consensus): ask directly about suicidal thoughts at intake and throughout treatment. Asking does not plant the idea. Assess ideation, plan, intent, access to means, past attempts, and protective factors. Use a structured tool (for example, the C-SSRS, or PHQ-9 item 9 as a screen), develop a collaborative safety plan, counsel on reducing access to lethal means, and consult or refer when risk is elevated. Watch especially for hopelessness, recent losses, agitation, substance use, and periods of transition (for example, early improvement in energy, or discharge).

4. What works: psychological treatments with the strongest evidence

Bottom line for class: several structured psychotherapies work for adult depression with broadly similar effects. CBT has by far the largest evidence base. Psychotherapy is about as effective as antidepressants in the short term, and combining the two beats either one alone for moderate-to-severe depression.

Cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT): the reference standard

Size of the evidence base. A meta-analysis of 409 trials (52,702 patients) found CBT had moderate-to-large effects against control conditions (g = 0.79). Its advantage over other psychotherapies was very small (g = 0.06) and was not robust. CBT matched antidepressants at the end of treatment and outperformed them at 6–12-month follow-up (g = 0.34). CBT combined with medication beat medication alone but did not beat CBT alone. CBT also worked as unguided self-help (g = 0.45) and in youth (g = 0.41) (Cuijpers et al., 2023).

Realistic expectations. Across 228 trials of psychotherapy for depression, about 41% of patients responded (50% symptom reduction), compared with about 17% under usual care. Only about one-third remitted. The NNT was about 5 versus usual care (Cuijpers et al., 2021). Teach students that many clients need more than one course of treatment, or a combination of treatments.

Behavioral activation (BA): the "behavioral core" of CBT

BA targets avoidance and withdrawal. Clients schedule valued, mastery, and pleasure activities, monitor how activity relates to mood, and solve problems around barriers.

In a large RCT, BA matched antidepressant medication among more severely depressed adults, and both outperformed cognitive therapy in that subgroup (Dimidjian et al., 2006).

A meta-analysis of 26 RCTs found BA superior to controls (SMD −0.74). Study quality was often low (Ekers et al., 2014).

The COBRA trial (n = 440) found BA delivered by junior mental health workers was non-inferior to CBT delivered by professional therapists at 12 months, at lower cost (Richards et al., 2016). This makes BA a strong, teachable first-line option.

Cognitive therapy (CT) and relapse prevention

CT (Beckian) identifies and tests negative automatic thoughts and underlying beliefs, alongside behavioral work. For people with recurrent depression who are in remission, mindfulness-based cognitive therapy (MBCT) reduced the risk of relapse over 60 weeks compared with usual care (HR 0.69) and compared with active treatments, including antidepressants (HR 0.79) (Kuyken et al., 2016).

Other therapies with strong evidence

Interpersonal psychotherapy (IPT) focuses on grief, role disputes, role transitions, and interpersonal deficits. It had moderate-to-large effects against controls (g = 0.60) and did not differ from other therapies or from pharmacotherapy. Maintenance IPT reduced relapse (Cuijpers et al., 2016).

Problem-solving therapy (PST) is effective. Pooled effects were g = 0.79, but only g = 0.34 in low-risk-of-bias trials, which is comparable to other therapies. That gap is a useful teaching example of how bias inflates effect sizes (Cuijpers et al., 2018).

Combined treatment. A network meta-analysis of 101 trials found psychotherapy plus medication more effective than either alone. Psychotherapy alone and medication alone did not differ, and psychotherapy was better tolerated (fewer dropouts) than medication (Cuijpers et al., 2020). Refer for a medication evaluation when depression is moderate-to-severe, chronic, psychotic, or not responding to treatment.

5. Christian and spiritually integrated interventions: what the evidence shows

Models. Religiously or spiritually integrated CBT keeps the standard CBT structure (thought records, behavioral activation, homework). It draws on the client's own faith resources: Scripture for challenging distorted thoughts, prayer and gratitude practices, service, forgiveness, and faith community as sources of activation and support. The best-known manualized protocol is Religiously Integrated CBT (RCBT) from Duke. It has versions for Christianity, Judaism, Islam, Buddhism, and Hinduism (Pearce et al., 2015).

Key findings:

Early RCT. Among religious adults with depression, religious CBT and pastoral counseling outperformed standard CBT and a waitlist after treatment. Effects were equal across active treatments at 3-month and 2-year follow-up. The samples were small (about 10–20 per arm) (Propst et al., 1992).

RCBT vs. conventional CBT. In 132 adults with MDD and chronic medical illness, the two produced equivalent outcomes overall. RCBT was slightly more effective, with somewhat better adherence, in more religious participants (Koenig et al., 2015).

Meta-analysis of faith-adapted CBT. Sixteen RCTs for depression and anxiety showed statistically significant benefits. However, trials were small and at high risk of bias, with researcher and therapist allegiance a particular concern. The authors concluded that firm recommendations require larger rigorous trials (Anderson et al., 2015).

Broadest meta-analysis. Across 97 studies, religiously or spiritually adapted therapy beat no treatment and modestly beat other secular therapies (psychological g = 0.33). In the most rigorous additive designs, which compare the same therapy with and without religious or spiritual content, results were equivalent on psychological distress (g = 0.13, not significant) but better on spiritual well-being (g = 0.34) (Captari et al., 2018).

Earlier reviews. Several religious or spiritual therapies were judged helpful, with limited evidence that they outperform established secular therapies. The choice "may be an issue of client preference and therapist comfort" (Hook et al., 2010). A review of RCTs across mental health outcomes found the clearest religious/spiritual intervention benefits for anxiety and more modest, time-limited effects on depression (Gonçalves et al., 2015).

Honest summary for teaching:

Christian- or faith-integrated CBT is about as effective as standard CBT for reducing depression in religious clients. It is not consistently superior.

Its added value appears in client fit, engagement, and adherence and in spiritual well-being, especially for highly religious clients.

The evidence base is smaller and weaker than for standard CBT: fewer trials, small samples, allegiance effects, and few trials that are specifically Christian and of high quality.

Practice ethics (general consensus): integrate faith only when the client prefers it and after a spiritual assessment. Stay within your competence and role. Do not substitute integration for evidence-based structure or risk assessment. Watch for spiritual struggles such as guilt, feeling punished, or anger at God, which can maintain depression.

6. Practical Christian-integration notes

These apply Dr. Campbell’s identify, challenge, replace-with-Scripture method within CBT and behavioral activation. Clinical items rest on the research above. Pastoral items are theological application, not research findings.

Identify (clinical). Listen for the core beliefs of depression (worthlessness, excessive guilt, hopelessness), often in spiritual form: "God is disappointed in me," "I am too far gone to be forgiven," "If I had enough faith, I would not be depressed" (Pearce et al., 2015). To identify deeper false beliefs, see: Core Beliefs, Parental Messages, or use the Semantic Search Tool.

Challenge (clinical). Test these beliefs with thought records and behavioral experiments, and rebuild valued activity through behavioral activation (Cuijpers et al., 2023; Dimidjian et al., 2006). Possible cognitive errors to watch for, with their definitions from the Belief Counseling Cognitive Errors Checklist and the depressive symptoms they tend to feed:

Global labeling — one mistake becomes who I am. Feeds: worthlessness. Sounds like: “I’m a failure.”

Emotional reasoning — I feel it, so it must be true. Feeds: depressed mood. Sounds like: “I feel worthless, so I am worthless.”

Disqualifying the positive — deciding good things don’t count. Feeds: low mood, loss of pleasure. Sounds like: “They were only being nice.”

Overgeneralization — one event becomes a rule. Feeds: depressed mood. Sounds like: “Nothing ever works out for me.”

Personalization — taking blame for things outside my control. Feeds: excessive or inappropriate guilt. Sounds like: “It’s my fault the family is struggling.”

Fortune telling — predicting a bad outcome. Feeds: hopelessness, thoughts of death. Sounds like: “It will never get better, so why try?” Always ask about suicide when you hear this.

Helplessness — outside forces control everything. Feeds: fatigue, low energy, withdrawal. Sounds like: “Nothing I do makes any difference.”

Unrealistic expectations — expecting too much of myself or others. Feeds: spiritual guilt. Sounds like: “A good Christian wouldn’t struggle like this.”

Questions to dispute the belief. Have the client choose questions from the Questioning False Beliefs form that “speak to” the belief. To see how, watch Dr. Campbell’s video Integrated Christian Counseling: Questioning Your Beliefs. Selected questions that fit the errors above:

“Doesn’t everyone have good points and bad points?” For: global labeling.

“What’s the actual evidence for my belief?” For: emotional reasoning.

“Have I overlooked some of the gifts that God has given me?” For: disqualifying the positive.

“Are there other ways of looking at this situation?” For: overgeneralization.

“Am I really the cause of the problem, or did someone just try to convince me that I am?” For: personalization and guilt.

“Can I really predict the future?” and “Has God given up hope that things will get better for me?” For: fortune telling and hopelessness.

“Am I absolutely certain that nothing can be done about this problem or situation?” For: helplessness.

“Is anyone perfect? Haven’t we all sinned and fallen short of the glory of God?” For: unrealistic expectations and spiritual guilt.

Replace (pastoral). Offer Scripture that answers condemnation and hopelessness with truth:

“Therefore, there is now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus.” (Romans 8:1). For excessive guilt and global labeling.

“Because of the loving devotion of the LORD we are not consumed, for His mercies never fail. They are new every morning; great is Your faithfulness!” (Lamentations 3:22–23). For hopelessness and fortune telling.

“The LORD is near to the brokenhearted; He saves the contrite in spirit.” (Psalm 34:18). Depression is not a sign of God’s absence.

“Do not be conformed to this world, but be transformed by the renewing of your mind…” (Romans 12:2). Changing thinking habits is itself a spiritual discipline.

Faith community (clinical). Steady companionship, shared meals, and small acts of service can serve as behavioral activation (Dimidjian et al., 2006). Encourage gently, without pressure or shame.

Ministers: never tell a depressed person that depression proves weak faith, take every mention of suicide seriously, and support referral for medication evaluation when depression is moderate to severe (Cuijpers et al., 2020).

7. Quick classroom takeaways

Diagnose on duration (≥2 weeks), count (≥5 of 9), core symptom (mood or anhedonia), and impairment. Always rule out bipolar disorder and assess suicide risk.

Screen and track with a brief free tool such as the PHQ-9; use the C-SSRS for suicide risk. Fuller tests such as the MMPI-3 and MCMI-IV require advanced qualification.

First-line psychotherapies are CBT, BA, IPT, and PST. Use MBCT or continued CBT for relapse prevention. Combine with medication for moderate-to-severe cases.

BA is simple, scalable, and as effective as full CBT.

Faith-integrated CBT is a legitimate, client-centered option for religious clients. It performs about as well as standard CBT, with added spiritual benefits, but the evidence is modest.
