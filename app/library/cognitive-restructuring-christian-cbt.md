# Cognitive Restructuring and Its Adaptation in Christian Cognitive-Behavioral Therapy: A Teaching Review

*Clinical Companion Knowledge Library: for counselors and counseling students*

**Date of review:** October 28, 2026

**Prepared for:** Brian Campbell, counselor education and clinical training (Liberty University context)

**Focus:** What cognitive restructuring is, how it works, how it is adapted in Christian CBT (identify → challenge → replace with the truth of Scripture), and what the research does and does not show.

---

## 1. Introduction and purpose

Few techniques in contemporary counseling are as widely taught, or as widely misunderstood, as cognitive restructuring. Students often meet it first as a worksheet: a thought record with columns for the situation, the "hot" thought, the evidence, and a "balanced" alternative. Experienced clinicians know it is more than a form. Done well, it is a collaborative, curious, and compassionate way of helping a person notice how they are interpreting their life, test those interpretations against reality, and hold conclusions that are more accurate and more livable.

For Christian counselors, and for Christian clients who want their faith honored in treatment, cognitive restructuring has a natural resonance. Scripture speaks often about the mind: being "transformed by the renewing of your mind" (Romans 12:2), taking "every thought captive" (2 Corinthians 10:5), and dwelling on what is "true… honorable… just… pure" (Philippians 4:8). A three-step teaching model summarizes how many Christian clinicians practice: **identify** the distorted thought, **challenge** it, and **replace it with the truth**, drawing on Scripture. This review sets out the secular foundations of cognitive restructuring first, then describes how Christian CBT and religiously integrated CBT adapt those foundations, maps the three-step model onto both, summarizes the empirical research honestly, and closes with ethical and practical guidance for students.

## 2. What cognitive restructuring is

### 2.1 Origins in the cognitive model

Cognitive restructuring is the core change procedure of cognitive therapy as developed by Aaron T. Beck and colleagues, and it remains central to the broader family of cognitive-behavioral therapies (Beck, 1963; Beck et al., 1979; Clark, 2014). Beck's early clinical observations of depressed patients identified a recurring pattern: their emotional distress was tied to idiosyncratic, negatively biased interpretations of themselves, their world, and their future, the "cognitive triad" (Beck, 1963; Beck et al., 1979). A parallel tradition, Albert Ellis's rational-emotive therapy, emphasized disputing irrational beliefs. Both traditions share the premise that **it is not events alone but the meanings people assign to events that shape emotion and behavior**.

The cognitive model, as it is usually taught today, describes three levels of cognition (J. S. Beck, 2021):

- **Automatic thoughts:** quick, situation-specific thoughts or images that seem to "pop up" without deliberate reasoning ("I'm going to fail this exam," "She thinks I'm boring"). They are usually believed in the moment and are closely tied to emotion.
- **Intermediate beliefs:** the rules, attitudes, and assumptions that link core beliefs to daily life, often in "if… then" form ("If I don't do everything perfectly, I'm a failure"; "I should never let anyone down").
- **Core beliefs (schemas):** deep, global, rigid beliefs about the self, others, and the world, often formed early ("I am unlovable," "I am helpless," "The world is dangerous"). J. S. Beck (2021) groups negative self-beliefs broadly into helpless, unlovable, and worthless clusters.

When a core belief is activated by a situation, it biases attention, interpretation, and memory, generating automatic thoughts that feel true and that, in turn, drive emotion, physiological arousal, and behavior (for example, avoidance or withdrawal). The behavior then often produces experiences that seem to confirm the belief, and the cycle continues.

### 2.2 Cognitive distortions

Beck (1963) described systematic errors in the thinking of depressed patients, and later clinical writers expanded these into the familiar list of **cognitive distortions** taught to clients. Common examples include all-or-nothing thinking, catastrophizing, overgeneralization, mental filtering (attending only to the negative), discounting the positive, mind reading, fortune telling, emotional reasoning ("I feel worthless, so I must be worthless"), "should" statements, labeling, and personalization (J. S. Beck, 2021). Naming the distortion is not the goal in itself. It helps clients step back from a thought and see it as a mental event that can be examined rather than as a fact.

### 2.3 The steps of cognitive restructuring

Across manuals and textbooks, cognitive restructuring moves through a recognizable sequence (J. S. Beck, 2021; Clark, 2014):

1. **Identify** the automatic thought (and, over time, the underlying assumptions and core beliefs). The counselor asks, "What was going through your mind just then?", especially at moments of shifting emotion. Clients learn to catch thoughts through self-monitoring.
2. **Evaluate (challenge)** the thought. The counselor and client examine it collaboratively: What is the evidence for and against it? Is there another explanation? What is the worst, best, and most realistic outcome? What would I tell a friend? What is the effect of believing this thought, and what might change if I thought differently?
3. **Generate an alternative (balanced or adaptive) thought** that better fits the evidence, then rate how much the client believes it and how the emotion changes. The replacement is not forced positivity. It is a more accurate, more complete, and more useful appraisal.
4. **Test and consolidate** through behavioral experiments and repeated practice between sessions, so that the new appraisal is learned in experience and not only in the counseling room.

### 2.4 Socratic questioning and collaborative empiricism

The spirit of cognitive restructuring is **guided discovery through Socratic questioning**. The counselor does not argue the client out of a belief. Instead, the counselor asks open, curious questions that help the client gather and weigh information and reach conclusions for themselves. Beck called this stance **collaborative empiricism**: counselor and client work as a team of investigators, treating thoughts as hypotheses to be tested (Beck et al., 1979). This matters empirically as well as philosophically. In a process study of 55 adults receiving cognitive therapy for depression, observer-rated therapist use of Socratic questioning in early sessions predicted session-to-session decreases in depressive symptoms, even after controlling for the therapeutic alliance (Braun et al., 2015).

### 2.5 Thought records, homework, and behavioral experiments

The **thought record** is the workhorse of cognitive restructuring. A typical version records the situation, the emotion and its intensity (0–100), the automatic thought and the degree of belief, evidence for and against, an alternative response, and a re-rating of belief and emotion (J. S. Beck, 2021). Between-session practice is not optional decoration. A meta-analysis of 46 studies (N = 1,072) found that homework assignments enhanced outcomes, with a pooled effect of d = 0.48 favoring homework when the same therapy was compared with and without it (Kazantzis et al., 2010). Clients' growing competence in using cognitive skills on their own has been linked to lower relapse risk: among 35 responders to cognitive therapy for depression, both coping-skill competence and in-session evidence of independent use of CT skills predicted lower relapse risk over the following year (Strunk et al., 2007).

## 3. Does cognitive restructuring work? A brief evidence summary

### 3.1 CBT efficacy in general

CBT, of which cognitive restructuring is a defining element, is among the most extensively studied psychotherapies. A review of 106 meta-analyses found the strongest support for CBT in anxiety disorders, somatoform disorders, bulimia, anger control problems, and general stress, while also noting gaps in research on specific subgroups such as ethnic minorities and low-income samples (Hofmann et al., 2012). For depression, the largest meta-analysis to date (409 trials, 52,702 patients) found moderate-to-large effects of CBT compared with control conditions (g = 0.79), effects that remained significant at 6–12 months, and long-term effects that compared favorably with pharmacotherapy in the smaller number of trials with follow-up (Cuijpers et al., 2023).

### 3.2 Is it the cognitive restructuring that helps?

Here the honest teaching point is "it's complicated." In a well-known component analysis, 150 outpatients with major depression were randomized to behavioral activation alone, behavioral activation plus work on automatic thoughts, or full cognitive therapy. The full package did not outperform its components at the end of acute treatment or at 6-month follow-up, and behavioral activation alone changed negative thinking about as much as full CT did (Jacobson et al., 1996). Reviews of component and mediation studies have concluded that the specific added value of direct thought challenging has not been clearly demonstrated (Longmore & Worrell, 2007). Clark's (2014) review similarly concluded that cognitive restructuring is an effective strategy, especially for anxiety and depression, and that it works by producing cognitive change, but that superiority over "less cognitive" interventions has not been shown.

Other researchers have read the same literature differently. Lorenzo-Luaces et al. (2015) argued that the methodological limits of existing studies make strong negative conclusions premature, and that the overall pattern supports two claims: cognitive procedures are effective at relieving depression, and **cognitive change, however it is achieved, contributes to symptom change**. For students, the practical lesson is balanced. Cognitive restructuring is a well-supported tool. It is most powerful when combined with behavioral work, practiced between sessions, and delivered in a warm, collaborative relationship. It is not a magic formula. Behavioral experiments and activity are often how beliefs actually change.

## 4. Christian CBT and religiously integrated CBT

### 4.1 Why adapt cognitive restructuring for Christian clients?

Many clients hold religious commitments that shape how they understand suffering, identity, sin, forgiveness, hope, and meaning. Some prefer counseling that explicitly honors those commitments; others worry that a secular counselor will dismiss their faith. Cognitive restructuring is unusually easy to integrate with faith because it already works at the level of beliefs and meanings, and because a Christian client's worldview offers a rich, already-trusted source of alternative appraisals. Tan (1987) and later writers argued that a biblical approach could retain CBT's empirical methods while broadening its scope: attending to the spiritual dimension, to the role of the Holy Spirit and prayer, and to truth as defined by Scripture rather than only by personal evidence.

At the same time, early Christian integrators also noted a common clinical frustration: clients often "know the truth" intellectually but cannot yet relate to it in a way that changes how they feel or live. Craigie and Tan (1989) described approaches for assessing core assumptions carefully and for using behavioral strategies, social support, imagery, and prayer as vehicles for experiencing God's truth more deeply. That observation remains a key teaching point: the "replace" step is not complete when the client can recite a verse. It is complete when the truth is believed, rehearsed, and lived.

### 4.2 What changes in Christian CBT, and what stays the same

Most Christian or religiously integrated CBT protocols keep the structure of standard CBT (agenda setting, psychoeducation, thought monitoring, Socratic evaluation, behavioral activation, homework, relapse prevention) and add explicitly religious content. The best-documented example is **Religiously Integrated Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (RCBT)**, a manualized protocol developed by Pearce, Koenig, and colleagues at Duke University for depression in people with chronic medical illness (Pearce et al., 2015). RCBT was written in versions for Christianity, Judaism, Islam, Buddhism, and Hinduism. In the Christian version, the counselor helps clients identify unhelpful thoughts and challenge and replace them using the client's own religious beliefs, Scripture, and practices such as prayer, gratitude, forgiveness, and service (Pearce et al., 2015). Pearce's (2016) practitioner primer translates this into seven Christian CBT tools for depression, beginning with **"Renewing Your Mind: Planting Truth"** (identifying and replacing unhelpful thoughts with scriptural truth) and **"Changing Your Mind: Metanoia,"** followed by redemptive reframing, reaching out and connecting, acceptance and forgiveness, gratitude, and service.

Common Christian adaptations of cognitive restructuring include:

- **Scripture as a source of alternative thoughts.** Biblically congruent truths become candidate replacement thoughts, for example "There is therefore now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus" (Romans 8:1) in place of "I'm beyond forgiveness" (Garzon, 2005; Pearce, 2016).
- **Scripture as a lens for evaluating thoughts.** Alongside "What is the evidence?", the counselor can ask, "How does this thought fit with what you believe God says about you?" or "Would Jesus describe you this way?"
- **Prayer.** Prayer can be part of the change process (bringing a fear to God, praying through a replacement truth), and some approaches use contemplative or inner-healing prayer with consenting clients (Tan, 2007).
- **Christian worldview assumptions.** Hope, grace, the reality of suffering in a fallen world, God's presence in suffering, and an identity grounded in being loved by God rather than in performance can reframe intermediate beliefs and core beliefs ("My worth depends on my achievements" becomes "My worth is given, not earned").
- **Religious behavioral experiments and homework.** Memorizing and meditating on Scripture, keeping a gratitude journal, serving others, reconnecting with a faith community, or acts of forgiveness (Pearce, 2016).
- **Religious imagery.** Imagining Christ present in a painful situation was one of the earliest tested adaptations (Propst, 1980).

What does **not** change is the stance. The counselor still works collaboratively and Socratically, the client still does the evaluating, and the counselor still respects the client's autonomy, including the client's own denominational convictions.

### 4.3 Distorted religious cognitions

Christian CBT must also address the fact that religious beliefs can be distorted. Clients sometimes hold religious cognitions that are inaccurate even within their own tradition and that worsen depression or anxiety: "God is punishing me," "If I really had faith, I wouldn't be anxious," "I've committed the unforgivable sin," "God has abandoned me," or "A good Christian never feels angry." These overlap with what researchers call **religious and spiritual struggles**, measured along six dimensions: divine, demonic, interpersonal, moral, doubt, and ultimate meaning (Exline et al., 2014). Spiritual struggles matter clinically. In the RCBT trial, higher baseline spiritual struggles predicted a slower decline in depression in both religious and conventional CBT (Pearce & Koenig, 2016). Christian cognitive restructuring is well suited to these struggles, because it lets the counselor examine a distorted theological belief using the client's own fuller theology, such as grace, lament, God's patience, and the honesty of the Psalms, rather than simply dismissing it.

## 5. Mapping the three-step model: identify, challenge, replace with the truth

The three-step model taught in this program (identify, challenge, replace with the truth of Scripture) maps directly onto the standard cognitive restructuring sequence while making the source of "truth" explicit.

| Step | Standard cognitive restructuring | Christian CBT adaptation | Counselor prompts |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Identify** | Catch automatic thoughts at moments of emotional shift; link them to situations, emotions, and behaviors; over time uncover intermediate rules and core beliefs; label distortions. | Same, plus attention to religious content in thoughts (for example, beliefs about God, sin, worth, or suffering); assess religious coping and spiritual struggles; ask how the client's faith relates to the problem. | "What went through your mind?" "What does that say about you?" "Where is God in that thought?" |
| **2. Challenge** | Socratic evaluation of evidence, alternative explanations, decatastrophizing, cost–benefit analysis, the "friend" test, behavioral experiments. | Same Socratic process, with an added question: "Is this thought consistent with what you believe Scripture teaches?" Distorted religious beliefs are examined against the client's broader theology. Prayer can be included with consent. | "What's the evidence?" "What would you say to a friend in your church?" "Does this match what you believe God says about you?" |
| **3. Replace with the truth** | Generate a balanced, evidence-based alternative thought; rate belief and emotion; rehearse; test in experiments; practice as homework. | Generate a replacement that is both realistic and biblically congruent, often anchored in a specific passage (for example, Romans 8:1, Psalm 139:14, Isaiah 41:10, Philippians 4:6–7). Rehearse through Scripture memory or meditation, prayer, worship, and community, then live it out in behavioral experiments. | "What truth do you want to stand on here?" "How much do you believe it now (0–100)?" "How could you practice this truth this week?" |

Three teaching cautions keep this model clinically sound:

1. **"Truth" should be realistic as well as scriptural.** A good replacement thought fits both the client's faith and the facts of the situation. "God will make sure I never fail" is neither accurate theology nor a safe prediction. "Even if I fail this exam, I am not a failure, and God is with me" (compare Isaiah 41:10) is both.
2. **Scripture should open the conversation, not close it.** Quoting a verse to correct a client can feel like spiritual pressure, especially to a client who already feels condemned. The most effective use is Socratic: the client finds, reads, and reflects on the passage, and judges how it speaks to the thought (Garzon, 2005).
3. **Replacement takes rehearsal.** Because "knowing the truth" is not the same as believing it at a gut level (Craigie & Tan, 1989), replacement thoughts should be rated, practiced daily, tested in action, and revisited when belief drops.

### 5.1 A brief illustration (composite, fictional)

A 24-year-old graduate student, a committed Christian, presents with depressed mood after a poor practicum evaluation. **Identify:** On a thought record she writes, "I'm a fraud. God must be disappointed in me. I should quit." Mood: sad 85, ashamed 90. Her counselor helps her see all-or-nothing thinking, labeling, and fortune telling, and they uncover an intermediate rule: "If I'm not excellent, I'm worthless to God and others." **Challenge:** Through Socratic questions she notes the evidence against "fraud" (two strong prior evaluations, specific and fixable feedback) and considers what she would tell a classmate. Asked how her thought fits her faith, she reflects that she believes grace is not earned, and that she has never thought God loved others in proportion to their performance. **Replace:** Together they write, "I received hard feedback in two areas I can grow in. My worth isn't my evaluation. I'm God's workmanship, still being formed (Ephesians 2:10), and I can take the next step." She rates belief at 60 and shame at 45. For homework she meditates on the passage each morning, prays about her fear, and schedules a meeting with her supervisor as a behavioral experiment. Over subsequent sessions the deeper rule about earned worth becomes the focus.

## 6. Research on religiously integrated and Christian CBT

### 6.1 Early Christian trials (1980s–1990s)

The empirical literature on Christian cognitive therapy began with small but carefully designed studies. Propst (1980) compared religious and nonreligious imagery for mildly depressed religious individuals and reported that the religious imagery condition produced greater treatment gains than nonreligious imagery or self-monitoring alone. Pecheur and Edwards (1984) randomized depressed Christian college students to secular cognitive therapy, a religious version, or a waitlist. Both active treatments outperformed the waitlist, with no significant difference between them, and gains held at one-month follow-up.

The most cited trial is Propst et al. (1992). Fifty-nine religious patients with nonpsychotic, nonbipolar depression received 18–20 sessions of religious CBT, standard CBT, pastoral counseling, or a waitlist, delivered by both religious and nonreligious therapists. Religious CBT and pastoral counseling produced lower posttreatment depression than standard CBT or the waitlist. Strikingly, the advantage was driven largely by **nonreligious therapists delivering the religious protocol**, and the three active treatments were equivalent at 3-month and 2-year follow-up (Propst et al., 1992). For students, the takeaway is that a structured religious protocol can help a counselor connect with a client's values even when the counselor does not share them.

Johnson and colleagues tested Christian versions of rational-emotive therapy, the disputation-focused cousin of cognitive restructuring. In an exploratory study, both Christian and standard brief RET reduced depression and automatic negative thoughts, and only the Christian version reduced clients' irrational beliefs (Johnson & Ridley, 1992). A follow-up comparative outcome study found that both treatments reduced depression, automatic negative thoughts, and irrational thinking, with gains maintained at three months and no differential effects (Johnson et al., 1994). In an inpatient program, Hawkins et al. (1999) compared Christian CBT (n = 18) with traditional CBT (n = 11) for depressed Christian adults. Both groups improved significantly in depression and spiritual well-being, and the Christian CBT group showed higher overall spiritual well-being, although the groups were small and unequal in size.

### 6.2 The Duke RCBT trial (2010s)

The largest Christian-inclusive trial of cognitive restructuring to date tested RCBT against conventional CBT in 132 adults with major depression and chronic medical illness. Participants received ten 50-minute sessions over 12 weeks, 94% by telephone (Koenig et al., 2015). The intention-to-treat analysis found **no significant difference** in depression outcome between RCBT and conventional CBT. Response and remission rates were similar. Overall religiosity interacted with treatment, suggesting RCBT was slightly more effective for more religious participants, and adherence to RCBT tended to be higher among the more religious (85.7% vs. 65.9%, a nonsignificant trend). The authors described the two treatments as equivalent (Koenig et al., 2015). Secondary analyses of the same trial found that both treatments reduced spiritual struggles to a similar degree, and that higher baseline struggles slowed depression response in both (Pearce & Koenig, 2016). Both also reduced suicidal thoughts comparably, with baseline religiosity predicting decreases independent of treatment group (Ramos et al., 2018).

### 6.3 Christian contemplative adaptations

Some Christian clinicians have adapted the "third wave" of CBT, which relates to thoughts by noticing and releasing them rather than disputing them, using Christian contemplative practice. In a two-site randomized trial at Christian universities, a 4-week manualized Christian meditation program for Christians with repetitive negative thinking (n = 36) outperformed a waitlist (n = 35) on perseverative thinking and nonattachment (large effects) and on humility and surrender to God (medium effects) (Knabb et al., 2020). This is a preventive study in a nonclinical sample, so it should be read as promising rather than definitive. It is also a reminder that "replacing" a thought is not the only faith-congruent way to change one's relationship with it.

### 6.4 Meta-analyses and systematic reviews

Meta-analytic findings across religious and spiritual (R/S) adaptations are consistent and should be taught carefully:

- **McCullough (1999)** pooled five studies (N = 111) comparing standard and religion-accommodative counseling for depression and found no evidence that either was more or less efficacious. He concluded that the choice is probably more a matter of client preference than of differential efficacy.
- **Smith et al. (2007)** meta-analyzed 31 outcome studies of spiritually oriented therapies (1984–2005) and reported an average random-effects effect size of 0.56.
- **Hook et al. (2010)** evaluated R/S therapies against empirically-supported-treatment criteria across many problems. They found several helpful R/S therapies but limited evidence that they outperform established secular treatments, again framing the choice as one of client preference and therapist comfort.
- **Worthington et al. (2011)** analyzed 51 samples (N = 3,290). R/S psychotherapies outperformed alternative secular psychotherapies on psychological (d = .26) and spiritual (d = .41) outcomes. In tighter dismantling designs, religiously accommodated treatments outperformed on spiritual outcomes (d = .33) but **not** on psychological outcomes.
- **Captari et al. (2018)**, the most comprehensive meta-analysis to date, included 97 outcome studies (N = 7,181). R/S-adapted psychotherapies produced large improvements compared with no treatment (g = 0.74 for psychological and spiritual functioning) and outperformed non-R/S psychotherapies overall (psychological g = 0.33; spiritual g = 0.43). In the most rigorous additive designs, where the same treatment was tested with and without R/S content, R/S-accommodated therapies were **equally effective** for psychological distress (g = 0.13, not significant) and produced **greater spiritual well-being** (g = 0.34).
- **Anderson et al. (2015)** reviewed 16 randomized trials of faith-adapted therapies for depression or anxiety. Notably, all used cognitive or cognitive-behavioral models. They found statistically significant benefits of faith-adapted CBT but rated the trials as small and susceptible to bias, especially researcher and therapist allegiance, and called for larger, rigorous trials before firm recommendations.
- **Lim et al. (2014)** reviewed nine randomized trials and one quasi-experimental study of religiously modified CBT for depression, generalized anxiety, and schizophrenia. Most found no difference from standard CBT or only early effects that were not sustained. They concluded that religiously modified CBT cannot yet be considered "well established" by formal criteria, but may be an acceptable option for clients with strong religious convictions.

In naturalistic practice, a study of 220 clients and 51 therapists in six Christian agencies and one secular agency found that clients in Christian and secular therapy improved comparably and felt equally close to their therapists. Across agencies, **clients with high religious commitment** reported greater closeness and greater improvement when religious interventions were used (Wade et al., 2007).

### 6.5 What the evidence supports, and where it is thin

Taken together, the evidence supports several conclusions that students can state with confidence:

1. Christian and religiously integrated forms of cognitive restructuring are **at least as effective** as standard CBT for reducing depression in religious clients. They are not reliably more effective on symptoms.
2. They tend to produce **added spiritual benefit**, such as spiritual well-being, which is a legitimate outcome for clients who value it.
3. Benefits may be larger for **more religious clients**, and adherence may be better when treatment fits clients' values (Koenig et al., 2015; Wade et al., 2007).
4. The research base is **small, older in places, and methodologically limited**. Many trials are small, allegiance effects are a concern, and most studies involve depression rather than anxiety, trauma, or other conditions (Anderson et al., 2015; Lim et al., 2014).

Two gaps deserve specific mention. First, I did not find rigorous trials that isolate **Scripture-based replacement thoughts** as a component, that is, comparing Scripture-anchored replacement with secular balanced thoughts while holding everything else constant. Claims that the Scripture step specifically drives outcomes therefore go beyond the data, even though the overall Christian CBT package is supported. Second, popular pastoral and lay "truth-replacement" models (for example, ministry-based discipleship-counseling programs) are widely used in churches but, as far as this review could determine, have **not** been tested in peer-reviewed randomized trials. They should not be presented to students as evidence-based on the strength of the Christian CBT literature. The meta-analysis by Anderson et al. (2015) concerns faith-adapted *CBT* delivered in research settings, not those programs.

## 7. Ethical and competent practice with religious clients

Integrating faith into cognitive restructuring requires the same ethical care as any other intervention, and some additional care besides.

- **Informed consent and client choice.** Explicit religious interventions (Scripture, prayer in session) should be offered, explained, and agreed to, not assumed. Pearce (2016) recommends confirming that the client both practices their faith and wants it used in therapy before beginning Christian CBT.
- **Assessment first.** Brief spiritual assessment clarifies the client's tradition, how central faith is, whether faith is a resource or a source of struggle, and which practices are meaningful (Exline et al., 2014; Vieten et al., 2013).
- **Avoid imposing values.** The ACA Code of Ethics (American Counseling Association, 2014) directs counselors not to impose their own values, attitudes, beliefs, and behaviors on clients. Christian counselors serving clients who do not want religious content should provide competent standard CBT.
- **Competence.** Spiritual and religious competencies have been articulated for psychologists, 16 in all (Vieten et al., 2013), and for counselors through the ASERVIC competencies (Cashwell & Watts, 2010). These include self-awareness of one's own beliefs, knowledge of diverse traditions, and skill in assessment and integration.
- **Theological humility and denominational sensitivity.** Use the client's own understanding of Scripture where possible. When a client's religious belief appears distorted, explore it Socratically ("Where does that idea come from? What do you believe about God's grace?") rather than adjudicating doctrine. Consult or collaborate with the client's pastor when appropriate and with consent.
- **Guard against spiritual bypass and harm.** Scripture should never be used to minimize suffering, shame clients for symptoms, discourage indicated medical care, or replace safety planning. Lament (for example, Psalms 13, 42–43) is a biblical model for honest expression of pain, not a failure of faith.
- **Scope of practice.** Counselors are not acting as clergy. Integration is a clinical decision made for the client's benefit and documented as such.

## 8. Practical recommendations for students and counselors

- Learn **standard cognitive restructuring thoroughly first.** Christian CBT is an adaptation of a skill, not a substitute for it.
- Keep the **Socratic, collaborative stance** at every step, including when Scripture is used.
- Build a **"truth column"** into thought records for consenting Christian clients: situation, emotion, automatic thought, distortion, evidence, *truth I will stand on (Scripture)*, balanced response, and re-rated belief and emotion.
- Anchor replacement thoughts in **specific passages chosen with the client**, and pair them with action: behavioral activation, experiments, service, and connection to a faith community.
- Address **distorted religious beliefs and spiritual struggles directly**, because they predict slower recovery (Pearce & Koenig, 2016).
- Measure both **symptom outcomes** (for example, PHQ-9, GAD-7) and, where relevant, **spiritual outcomes**. The research suggests the spiritual gains are where religious adaptation adds most.
- Present the evidence **accurately to clients and colleagues**: Christian CBT works as well as standard CBT on symptoms, may suit religious clients better, and is supported by a modest but growing literature.

## 9. Conclusion

Cognitive restructuring rests on a simple, durable insight: how we interpret our experience shapes how we feel and act, and interpretations can be examined and changed. The Beckian sequence (identify automatic thoughts, evaluate them through guided discovery, and develop balanced alternatives) is well supported as part of CBT, even as researchers debate exactly how much the cognitive component adds beyond behavioral change. Christian CBT keeps this sequence and gives the third step an explicit anchor: the client's trusted source of truth in Scripture, practiced through prayer, meditation, community, and obedience. The three-step model of identify, challenge, and replace with the truth is a faithful and clinically coherent summary of that adaptation. The evidence suggests that, for religious clients who want it, faith-integrated cognitive restructuring is as effective as standard CBT for symptoms and offers added spiritual benefit. Delivered with consent, competence, humility, and Socratic respect, it allows counselors to help clients renew their minds in a way that honors both good science and their deepest commitments.
