Belief Counseling · Clinical Practice Guidelines

Sexual Abuse

Counseling survivors of childhood sexual abuse and adult sexual assault

Dr. Brian Campbell

← All Guidelines

These guidelines apply the general Clinical Practice Guidelines to counseling people who were sexually abused as children or sexually assaulted as adults. Sexual abuse is common. The CDC reports that at least 1 in 4 girls and 1 in 20 boys in the United States experience child sexual abuse, and about 90% of it is committed by someone the child or family knows and trusts. In a national survey, about 1 in 4 women reported a completed or attempted rape in her lifetime. Many survivors come to counseling for depression, anxiety, marriage problems, or a faith crisis, and have never told anyone what happened.

Sexual abuse is a form of trauma and of abuse, so the Trauma and PTSD guidelines and the Abuse guidelines apply as well. Those pages cover current danger, safety planning, and couples counseling in detail. This page focuses on the wounds that are particular to sexual abuse: secrecy, false guilt, shame, memory, the body, and sexuality.

Please note: This is a practice framework, not a substitute for professional judgment, supervision, specialized training, the law, licensing-board rules, informed consent, or your profession’s ethics code. Reporting laws differ by state; know yours.

Keep these numbers at hand

  • Emergency: 911
  • 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline: call or text 988
  • RAINN National Sexual Assault Hotline (free, confidential, 24/7): 1-800-656-4673 (800-656-HOPE), chat at hotline.rainn.org, or text HOPE to 64673. Calls are routed to a local sexual assault service provider.
  • Childhelp National Child Abuse Hotline: 1-800-422-4453

The central principle
The shame belongs to the abuser, not the survivor.

Our goal is to believe and protect, lift the false guilt and shame that sexual abuse leaves behind, and help survivors replace its lies with the truth of their dignity and worth to God.

1Ask Gently, and Believe What You Hear

Identify

Sexual abuse is usually kept secret for years. In a national study of women raped before age 18, 28% had never told anyone before the research interview, and 47% had waited more than five years. Many survivors will only tell when they are asked by someone who seems safe.

In practice

  • Ask every client, in a calm and routine way, about unwanted sexual experiences in childhood and adulthood.
  • Let the client decide how much to say and when. Do not press for details early on.
  • When someone discloses, respond with belief and support: thank them, and tell them it was not their fault.
  • Watch your face and voice. Shock, disgust, or doubt can close the door for years.
  • Ask what the client needs from you now, and what they want to happen next.

Try saying

“Some people I see have had unwanted sexual experiences, as children or as adults. Has anything like that ever happened to you?”

“Thank you for trusting me with this. What happened was not your fault.”

“The LORD is near to the brokenhearted; He saves the contrite in spirit.” (Psalm 34:18, BSB)

2Protect Children Now, and Know the Law

Identify

Someone who abused one child may still be a danger to others. An adult survivor’s abuser may still have access to children: nieces, nephews, grandchildren, a youth group, or a classroom. And a recent assault raises urgent medical and legal questions.

In practice

  • If the client is a minor, follow your state’s mandatory reporting law.
  • When an adult describes past abuse, ask whether the abuser has access to children now. Know whether your state requires or permits a report in that case, and consult when unsure.
  • Explain the limits of confidentiality at the first session, before anything is disclosed.
  • After a recent assault, encourage prompt medical care. A sexual assault forensic exam may be available even if the survivor has not decided whether to report to police. RAINN can connect survivors with local help.
  • For adults, whether to report to police is the survivor’s choice. Support it either way.
  • Screen for current danger, especially if the abuser is a partner or family member, and follow the Abuse guidelines.

Try saying

“Does he still have contact with any children?”

“Defend the cause of the weak and fatherless; uphold the rights of the afflicted and oppressed.” (Psalm 82:3, BSB)

3Lift the False Guilt

Challenge

Almost every survivor blames herself or himself. “Why didn’t I fight?” “Why didn’t I tell?” “I went back.” “My body responded.” But in a study of women seen after sexual assault, 70% reported significant tonic immobility, an involuntary freeze, during the assault. Children are groomed with attention, gifts, and secrecy until abuse feels normal. Freezing, silence, and physical arousal are not consent.

In practice

  • Teach how the body reacts to terror: fight, flight, and freeze. Freezing is automatic, not a choice.
  • Explain grooming: how abusers build trust, test boundaries, and make children feel responsible.
  • Explain that the body can respond to touch even when the person does not want it. That response is not consent and not sin.
  • Gently ask questions that put responsibility where it belongs. Who was the adult? Who had the power? Who chose to abuse?
  • Remember that God’s law did not blame the young woman who was attacked in the field: she had committed no sin, because there was no one to save her (Deuteronomy 22:26–27).

Try saying

“You were a child, and he was an adult. Whose job was it to stop?”

“Your body froze to survive. That isn’t weakness, and it isn’t consent.”

“When he found her in the field, the betrothed woman cried out, but there was no one to save her.” (Deuteronomy 22:27, BSB)

4Replace the Lies Abuse Teaches

Identify · Challenge · Replace

This is where Belief Counseling helps most. Sexual abuse teaches deep lies about self, others, and God: “I’m dirty.” “I’m damaged goods.” “My only value is sexual.” “No one will protect me.” “All men are dangerous.” “God was not there, or did not care.” “If people knew, they would reject me.” These beliefs drive shame, depression, isolation, and unhealthy relationships long after the abuse ends.

In practice

  • Listen for the beliefs underneath the symptoms, and write them down in the client’s own words.
  • Examine them gently. “Where did you learn that? Who taught you that?”
  • Separate what was done to the client from who the client is.
  • Replace the lies with truth about dignity, worth, and being made in God’s image. Belief cards make good homework.
  • Expect the truth to feel false at first. Repetition and the counselor’s steady acceptance help it take root.

Try saying

“What happened to you was dirty. You are not.”

“Instead of shame, My people will have a double portion, and instead of humiliation, they will rejoice in their share; and so they will inherit a double portion in their land, and everlasting joy will be theirs.” (Isaiah 61:7, BSB)

5Use Trauma-Focused Treatment, Paced for Safety

Challenge

Sexual abuse often leads to PTSD, depression, anxiety, dissociation, substance use, eating problems, and self-harm. Trauma-focused treatments such as Cognitive Processing Therapy, Prolonged Exposure, and EMDR help adults, and Trauma-Focused CBT helps children and teens. Survivors of long-term childhood abuse may first need help with safety, stability, and coping skills.

In practice

  • Assess for PTSD, depression, dissociation, substance use, eating problems, self-harm, and suicidal thoughts.
  • Build stability first when it is needed: grounding, sleep, coping skills, and safe relationships.
  • When the client is ready, use a trauma-focused treatment you are trained in, or refer to someone who is.
  • Give the client control over the pace. Let them know they can pause or stop at any time.
  • See the Trauma and PTSD guidelines for more detail.

Try saying

“We will go at a pace you can handle. You are in charge of what we talk about and when.”

“He heals the brokenhearted and binds up their wounds.” (Psalm 147:3, BSB)

6Be Careful With Memory

All steps

Many survivors remember the abuse clearly but have gaps or blurry details, and that is normal. But no set of symptoms proves that someone was sexually abused. In the 1990s, some therapists used hypnosis, guided imagery, and repeated suggestion to “recover” memories, and some clients came to believe things that had not happened, with devastating results for families. Professional associations warn against these methods.

In practice

  • Work with what the client remembers. Do not tell a client that their symptoms mean they must have been abused.
  • Do not use hypnosis, guided imagery, dream interpretation, or repeated leading questions to search for hidden memories.
  • Ask open questions, and avoid supplying details or names.
  • Accept uncertainty. A client can heal without having every detail.
  • Keep careful, objective notes, using the client’s own words.

Try saying

“You don’t have to remember everything to heal. Let’s start with what you do know.”

“Even zeal is no good without knowledge, and he who hurries his footsteps misses the mark.” (Proverbs 19:2, BSB)

7Make Room for the Body and Sexuality

Replace

Sexual abuse wounds the body and the gift of sexuality. Survivors may feel numb, disgusted, or afraid during intimacy, have flashbacks during sex, avoid medical exams, or swing toward compulsive sexual behavior. Many Christian wives feel guilty that they cannot enjoy what God created as good.

In practice

  • Ask, with permission, how the abuse affects the client’s body, health care, and sexual relationship.
  • Teach that triggers during intimacy are trauma responses, not rejection of a spouse.
  • Never pressure a survivor toward sex to fulfill a duty. Healing needs safety, choice, and patience.
  • With the client’s consent, help a spouse understand triggers and how to respond with gentleness.
  • Refer to a trauma-informed sex therapist or physician when needed.

Try saying

“Your body learned to protect you. We can help it learn that it is safe now.”

“I praise You, for I am fearfully and wonderfully made. Marvelous are Your works, and I know this very well.” (Psalm 139:14, BSB)

8Do Not Let Anyone Silence the Survivor

All steps

When King David’s daughter Tamar was raped by her half-brother, her brother told her, “Be quiet for now.” Her father, King David, was furious, but Scripture records nothing he did for her. She lived the rest of her life desolate. Families and churches still silence survivors to protect a reputation, a marriage, or a ministry. Silence keeps survivors trapped and leaves abusers free.

In practice

  • Affirm the survivor’s right to tell the truth, and to choose whom to tell.
  • Help the client plan any family disclosure or confrontation carefully. It is the client’s choice, never a requirement for healing.
  • Never pressure forgiveness or reconciliation with an abuser. See the Abuse guidelines.
  • When abuse happened in a church, take it seriously. Spiritual leaders who abused or covered up abuse can make God feel unsafe.
  • Help survivors find safe people and, when they are ready, a church that believes and protects.

Try saying

“You were told to keep quiet. Here, you can say what really happened.”

“Her brother Absalom said to her, ‘Has your brother Amnon been with you? Be quiet for now, my sister. He is your brother. Do not take this thing to heart.’ So Tamar lived as a desolate woman in the house of her brother Absalom.” (2 Samuel 13:20, BSB)

9Remember Men and Boys

Identify

Boys are sexually abused too, and men often wait even longer to tell. Male survivors may fear they will not be believed, that the abuse means something about their sexuality or manhood, or that they will be seen as weak. Many have never been asked.

In practice

  • Ask men the same screening questions you ask women.
  • Address common male survivor beliefs: “Men can’t be victims.” “I should have stopped it.” “It means I’m gay.” “If I was aroused, I wanted it.”
  • Watch for anger, substance use, compulsive sexual behavior, and isolation, which can hide the pain.
  • Offer referrals to resources and groups for male survivors when available.

Try saying

“What happened to you was abuse. It doesn’t change who you are as a man.”

“Those who look to Him are radiant with joy; their faces shall never be ashamed.” (Psalm 34:5, BSB)

10Guard Boundaries and Care for Yourself

All steps

Sexual abuse is a betrayal of trust by someone with power. The counseling relationship also involves trust and power, so the counselor must be scrupulously safe. Hearing these stories week after week also affects the counselor.

In practice

  • Ask whether the client would feel safer with a male or female counselor, and honor that when you can.
  • Never touch a client without clear permission. Let the client choose where to sit and whether the door is open or closed.
  • Keep strict professional and sexual boundaries. Any sexual contact with a client is always wrong and deeply harmful.
  • Watch for signs of secondary trauma in yourself: intrusive images, numbness, irritability, or losing hope.
  • Get regular supervision or consultation, and care for your own body, soul, and relationships.

Try saying

“If anything I do makes you uncomfortable, please tell me, and I’ll change it.”

“The Spirit of the Lord GOD is on Me, because the LORD has anointed Me to preach good news to the poor. He has sent Me to bind up the brokenhearted, to proclaim liberty to the captives and freedom to the prisoners.” (Isaiah 61:1, BSB)

Common Missteps: What to Say Instead

AvoidTry instead
“Why didn’t you tell anyone?”“It makes sense that telling felt impossible. What made it hard?”
“Why didn’t you fight back?”“Your body froze to protect you. That is a normal reaction to terror.”
“Your symptoms sound like you were abused. Try to remember.”“Let’s work with what you remember. You don’t need every detail to heal.”
“You need to forgive him so you can move on.”“Forgiveness can come in its own time. Right now, your healing matters most.”
“As his wife, you shouldn’t refuse him.”“Healing needs safety and choice. Let’s talk about what feels safe for you.”
“That was a long time ago.”“What happened then is still affecting you now. That makes sense.”
“Let’s keep this within the family.”“You get to decide whom to tell. And if children are at risk, we need to protect them.”

A Quick Check for Sexual Abuse Counseling

  • Have I asked about unwanted sexual experiences, with men as well as women?
  • Did I respond to the disclosure with belief and support, without pressing for details?
  • Is any child at risk now? Do I know my reporting duties?
  • Am I helping the client place responsibility on the abuser, not on herself or himself?
  • Have I identified the lies about self, others, and God that the abuse taught?
  • Am I pacing trauma work for safety, and using a treatment I am trained in?
  • Am I careful not to suggest memories or details?
  • Have I avoided pressuring forgiveness, reconciliation, silence, or sex?
  • Am I keeping clear boundaries and getting consultation?

For Students

Practice asking screening questions and responding to a disclosure out loud until you can do it calmly. Learn your state’s reporting law, including what to do when an adult reports past abuse by someone who still has access to children. Read about memory and suggestion before you ever work with a survivor. Bring every sexual abuse case to supervision, and pay attention to how these stories affect you.
