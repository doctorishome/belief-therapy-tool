For Counselors

Is Christian CBT Ethical?

Evidence, insurance, and informed consent

By Brian Campbell, Ph.D.

Many Christian counselors want to bring their faith into the counseling room, and many Christian clients want exactly that. But careful counselors ask hard questions. Is there scientific support for integrating faith and counseling? Will insurance companies accept it? And how do we honor clients who do not share our faith? I believe Christian Cognitive-Behavioral Therapy (CCBT) gives a sound answer to all three. I used this approach throughout my 25 years of private practice in Christian counseling.

A note on terms: in this article, CCBT means Christian Cognitive-Behavioral Therapy. Some research articles use the same letters for “conventional” CBT, so read carefully when you see them in the literature.

What Christian CBT Is

Cognitive-Behavioral Therapy (CBT) combines cognitive techniques with behavioral techniques to help clients improve their mental health. The cognitive side helps clients identify and change inaccurate beliefs and replace them with truer, more helpful ones. The behavioral side uses well-established methods, such as behavioral experiments and activity scheduling, to help change both thinking and behavior.

Christian CBT uses the same techniques. The difference lies in the content. When a CCBT counselor helps a client replace a false belief, the truth that replaces it may come from Scripture as well as from evidence and reason. In other words, the form of therapy (the techniques) is essentially the same; only the content differs. Biblical truth takes its place alongside secular explanations as a source of the truthful thinking that replaces a lie.

In a very real sense, CCBT is an extension of CBT into the realm of faith.

The Question of Evidence

McMinn (2011) gave careful attention to whether integrated Christian counseling is ethical, given the lack of controlled research behind it at the time. He noted that “no religiously oriented interventions have been evaluated in two independent double-blind studies” (p. 23). That was a fair concern. Since then, the research picture has changed considerably.

  1. A meta-analysis of faith-adapted therapiesAnderson et al. (2015) reviewed randomized controlled trials of faith-adapted treatments for depression and anxiety. All 16 eligible studies used cognitive or cognitive-behavioral models, and faith-adapted CBT showed significant benefits. There was some suggestion that it may outperform standard CBT, though the authors cautioned that the studies had methodological limitations and that larger trials are needed.
  2. A randomized trial of religious CBTKoenig et al. (2015) randomized 132 adults with major depression and chronic medical illness to religiously integrated CBT or conventional CBT. The two treatments were equally effective, and the more religious participants tended to stay with religious CBT more consistently.
  3. A comprehensive meta-analysisCaptari et al. (2018) analyzed 97 outcome studies (7,181 clients) of therapies tailored to clients’ religious and spiritual beliefs. These therapies improved both psychological and spiritual functioning. Compared with secular versions of the same therapies, they produced similar psychological improvement and greater spiritual improvement.

Taken together, the research shows that adapting CBT to a client’s faith does not cost the client anything in effectiveness, and it may add real benefit for clients whose faith matters to them. We should be honest that the research is still growing. But the concern McMinn raised in 2011 has been substantially answered, especially for integration with CBT.

This matters because CBT itself is among the most thoroughly researched forms of psychotherapy, with strong evidence for depression, anxiety, and many other conditions. When we practice CCBT, we stand on that foundation.

Insurance and Treatment Plans

McMinn (2011) also predicted that insurance companies might soon require counseling to follow approved treatment protocols. That prediction has largely come true. Insurers typically expect evidence-based treatment, and CBT is one of the treatments they most readily accept.

In private practice, I often had to submit treatment plans for clients to receive benefits. I wrote those plans as CBT plans: the diagnosis, measurable goals, and the CBT interventions I would use, such as cognitive restructuring and behavioral experiments. That was an accurate description of the treatment. The fact that the truths used to correct a client’s false beliefs may come from Scripture does not change the method.

Two cautions apply. First, documentation must always describe what you actually do. If you use prayer, Scripture, or other religious practices at the client’s request, it is wise to note this in your records as part of the agreed treatment. Never present the treatment as something it is not. Second, services that are purely spiritual guidance, rather than treatment of a diagnosed condition, should not be billed as psychotherapy.

Ethical Safeguards

Using CCBT does not settle every ethical question by itself. What makes integrated counseling ethical is how the counselor practices it. Five safeguards matter most:

Informed consent
Before treatment begins, explain your approach and let the client choose between traditional CBT and Christian CBT (ACA, 2014, Standard A.2).
Respect for the client’s values
Never impose your own beliefs. Follow the client’s lead on how much faith to include, and offer the same quality of care to clients who choose secular CBT (ACA, 2014, Standard A.4.b).
Assessment first
Learn about the client’s faith background before using Scripture or prayer. For some clients, especially those hurt by a church, religious language can feel like pressure or condemnation rather than help.
Competence
Practice within your training, and seek supervision or consultation as you learn to integrate faith and therapy (ACA, 2014, Standard C.2.a).
Referral
When a client’s needs, or a conflict of values, go beyond what you can ethically provide, refer.

A Model for Informed Consent

In my practice, every new client read a Client Handbook and signed a Consent for Treatment form before the first session. The Handbook explained my approach this way:

Dr. Campbell is a licensed psychologist with extensive training and experience in a variety of treatment techniques. His main treatment approach is Cognitive-Behavioral Therapy (CBT), which may involve a variety of established, scientifically supported techniques.

Dr. Campbell is a practicing Christian, and he also specializes in providing CBT from a distinctly Christian perspective. This may include the use of Scripture, prayer, or other religiously oriented activities. Combining Christian content with traditional CBT is called Christian Cognitive-Behavioral Therapy (CCBT). At the start of treatment, Dr. Campbell will discuss with you whether you would prefer counseling based on traditional CBT or on CCBT.

You are welcome to adapt this wording for your own consent forms.

Conclusion

When counselors obtain informed consent, respect each client’s values, practice within their competence, and use CCBT, which rests on one of the most thoroughly researched forms of psychotherapy, I believe they are providing a high standard of care and working well within the ethical guidelines for counselors and psychologists.
