1. What it is

A substance use disorder (SUD) is a problematic pattern of using alcohol or another drug that continues despite clear harm. DSM-5-TR uses one set of 11 criteria across substances (alcohol, cannabis, opioids, stimulants, sedatives, and others) and rates severity by how many are met (American Psychiatric Association [APA], 2022).

How common: in the U.S., lifetime alcohol use disorder was 29.1% (13.9% in the past year), yet only 19.8% had ever been treated (Grant et al., 2015). Lifetime drug use disorder was 9.9% (3.9% in the past year) (Grant et al., 2016).

Rarely alone: across 22 surveys, people with SUDs had much higher odds of depression and anxiety; illicit drug use disorder carried nearly four times the odds of major depression (OR 3.80) (Lai et al., 2015).

Counselor framing: addiction is a treatable condition, not simply a moral failure. Most people with an SUD never receive treatment, so every counselor and pastor needs to screen for it.

2. Core symptoms to watch for

DSM-5-TR criteria, paraphrased (APA, 2022); use the manual itself for formal diagnosis. A problematic pattern causing distress or impairment, with 2 or more of 11 within 12 months:

  • Impaired control: using more or longer than intended; wanting or failing to cut down; much time spent getting, using, or recovering; craving.

  • Social impairment: failing major duties at work, school, or home; continuing despite relationship problems; giving up important activities.

  • Risky use: use in physically dangerous situations; continuing despite known physical or psychological harm.

  • Physical dependence: tolerance and withdrawal (these two do not count when a medication is taken as prescribed).

  • Severity: mild (2 to 3 criteria), moderate (4 to 5), severe (6 or more). Specifiers include early or sustained remission and "in a controlled environment."

Screening: the 3-question AUDIT-C detected heavy drinking as well as the full 10-item AUDIT (AUROC 0.891 vs. 0.881) (Bush et al., 1998). Ask about every substance, including prescription medications.

Suicide risk: suicide rates were about 10 times expected in alcohol use disorder, 13.5 times in opioid use disorder, and nearly 17 times with mixed drug use (Wilcox et al., 2004). Ask directly, especially during intoxication, withdrawal, and relapse.

When counselors must refer (general clinical consensus): refer for medical care when there is risk of dangerous withdrawal (alcohol, benzodiazepines), opioid use (overdose risk; medication treatment), pregnancy, suicidality, or serious medical or psychiatric illness.

4. What works: treatment and the evidence

Bottom line for class: several approaches work about equally well: CBT and relapse prevention, motivational interviewing, contingency management, and 12-step facilitation. For opioid and alcohol use disorders, medication adds real benefit, and for opioids it saves lives.

CBT (honest effect sizes):

  • Across 53 trials, CBT had a small overall effect (g = 0.15) that faded by 12 months, but a large effect against no treatment (g = 0.80) and for cannabis (g = 0.51) (Magill & Ray, 2009).

  • An updated meta-analysis found CBT beat minimal or no treatment by a moderate margin, but was not better than other specific therapies such as motivational or 12-step approaches (Magill et al., 2019).

  • Relapse prevention (identifying high-risk situations and coping with them) was effective, especially for alcohol and when combined with medication (Irvin et al., 1999).

Other approaches. Psychosocial treatments for drug use have moderate effects overall, and contingency management (rewards for negative drug tests) had the strongest (Dutra et al., 2008): d = 0.42 overall and about 0.65 for opioids and cocaine (Prendergast et al., 2006). Motivational interviewing produced small, durable effects against weak comparisons (g = 0.28) but was not better than other specific treatments (Lundahl et al., 2010).

12-step programs. In a Cochrane review of 27 studies (10,565 people), manualized AA and twelve-step facilitation improved continuous abstinence at 12 months compared with other treatments such as CBT (RR 1.21, high-certainty evidence) (Kelly et al., 2020).

Medication (referral notes). For alcohol, naltrexone (number needed to treat 12 to prevent heavy drinking) and acamprosate (12 to prevent any drinking) help (Jonas et al., 2014). In the COMBINE trial, naltrexone or a combined behavioral intervention each improved abstinence alongside medical management (Anton et al., 2006). For opioids, buprenorphine keeps people in treatment and at high doses reduces illicit use (Mattick et al., 2014); death rates were about 3 times higher out of methadone treatment than in it (Sordo et al., 2017). Never discourage medication for opioid use disorder.

CBT for SUDs: how it is used:

  • Build motivation before change; resolve ambivalence (Lundahl et al., 2010).

  • Map triggers and high-risk situations, and practice coping and refusal skills (Irvin et al., 1999).

  • Challenge permission-giving and hopeless beliefs: "I can handle just one," "I deserve it," "I can’t cope without it," "I’ve already blown it, so I might as well keep going" (Magill et al., 2019).

  • Plan for lapses so a slip does not become a full relapse, and connect the person to ongoing peer support (Kelly et al., 2020).

5. Religion, spirituality, and Christian integration: what the evidence shows

Faith is already part of most treatment. About 73% of U.S. addiction treatment programs include a spiritual element, mostly through 12-step programs that emphasize reliance on God or a Higher Power (Grim & Grim, 2019).

Spirituality helps explain how recovery works. In AA, attending meetings increased spiritual practices, and this growth partly explained AA’s better drinking outcomes (Kelly et al., 2011).

Intervention studies:

  • Spiritual and religious interventions overall (20 randomized trials, 3,700 people): a small but significant advantage over other treatments (d = 0.18). Because only 12-step approaches were compared head to head, this does not yet show that other faith-based treatments are superior (Hai et al., 2019).

  • Christian faith-based treatment (not randomized): compared with a secular program, residents of a Christian program grew more in religiosity, and that growth helped explain their abstinence 6 months after treatment (Yeung, 2022).

  • CBT in the church: a computer-delivered CBT program led by church health advisors in Black churches (n = 40) was feasible, with good retention and reduced substance use; a randomized trial is still needed (Jordan et al., 2021).

Honest summary for teaching:

  1. CBT, motivational interviewing, contingency management, 12-step facilitation, and (for alcohol and opioids) medication are the evidence base. Faith integration supports them; it does not replace them.

  2. Spiritual growth is one of the ways recovery works, and 12-step approaches have the strongest evidence among faith-oriented treatments.

  3. Christian-specific programs show promising but non-randomized results. Combine them with evidence-based care and medical treatment, never in place of it.

6. Practical Christian-integration notes

These apply Dr. Campbell’s identify, challenge, replace-with-Scripture method within CBT and relapse prevention. Clinical items rest on the research above. Pastoral items are theological application, not research findings.

  • Identify (clinical). Ask about faith as a resource and as a source of shame. Listen for beliefs such as "God could never forgive what I’ve done," "If I had enough faith I wouldn’t need treatment or medication," and "I’ve relapsed, so I’m not really a Christian." To identify deeper false beliefs, see: Core Beliefs, Parental Messages, or use the Semantic Search Tool.

  • Challenge (clinical). Test permission-giving thoughts and shame with evidence and a lapse plan. Correct the belief that medication for opioid use disorder is "trading one drug for another" (Mattick et al., 2014; Sordo et al., 2017). Possible cognitive errors to watch for, with their definitions from the Belief Counseling Cognitive Errors Checklist and the symptoms they tend to feed:

  • All-or-nothing thinking — no middle ground. Feeds: lapses turning into relapse. Sounds like: “I slipped once, so I’ve blown it; I might as well keep using.”

  • Emotional reasoning — I feel it, so it must be true. Feeds: giving in to cravings. Sounds like: “I need it so badly, I have to have it.”

  • Global labeling — one mistake becomes who I am. Feeds: shame. Sounds like: “I’m just an addict.”

  • Blaming others — others are responsible for how I feel. Feeds: continued use. Sounds like: “I only drink because of my wife.”

  • Fortune telling — predicting a bad outcome. Feeds: hopelessness. Sounds like: “I’ll never stay clean.”

  • Unrealistic expectations — expecting too much of myself or others. Feeds: refusing treatment. Sounds like: “If I had enough faith, I wouldn’t need treatment.”

  • Questions to dispute the belief. Have the client choose questions from the Questioning False Beliefs form that “speak to” the belief. To see how, watch Dr. Campbell’s video Integrated Christian Counseling: Questioning Your Beliefs. Selected questions that fit the errors above:

  • “Am I keeping things in perspective?” and “Are there things that I can learn from my mistakes?” For: all-or-nothing thinking.

  • “Will I shrivel up and die if this happens?” For: emotional reasoning about cravings.

  • “Doesn’t everyone have good points and bad points?” and “Are my sins really too big for the God of the universe to forgive?” For: global labeling and “God could never forgive me”.

  • “Hasn’t God given me free will and the ability to change?” For: blaming others.

  • “Am I completely destined to stay this way my whole life?” For: fortune telling.

  • “How does the Bible speak to this?” For: unrealistic expectations about treatment.

  • Replace (pastoral). Offer Scripture of freedom, forgiveness, and strength in temptation:

  • “No temptation has seized you except what is common to man. And God is faithful; He will not let you be tempted beyond what you can bear. But when you are tempted, He will also provide an escape, so that you can stand up under it.” (1 Corinthians 10:13). Build the “escape” into the relapse plan.

  • “Therefore if anyone is in Christ, he is a new creation. The old has passed away. Behold, the new has come!” (2 Corinthians 5:17)

  • “Because of the loving devotion of the LORD we are not consumed, for His mercies never fail. They are new every morning; great is Your faithfulness!” (Lamentations 3:22–23). A lapse is not the end.

  • “Therefore confess your sins to each other and pray for each other so that you may be healed.” (James 5:16). Accountability and peer support.

  • Faith community (clinical). Connect the person with a recovery group, such as AA or a church-based recovery ministry, and a sponsor or accountability partner (Kelly et al., 2020; Kelly et al., 2011).

  • Ministers: screen with the AUDIT-C, respond without shame, and refer for medical assessment before anyone stops alcohol, sedatives, or opioids suddenly.

7. Quick classroom takeaways

  • Diagnose by 2 or more of 11 criteria in a year; rate mild, moderate, or severe. Screen everyone (AUDIT-C), and ask about suicide and overdose risk.

  • CBT, motivational interviewing, contingency management, and 12-step facilitation all work; medication is essential for opioid use disorder and helpful for alcohol use disorder.

  • Faith and fellowship are part of how recovery works. Replace shame and "I’ve blown it" thinking with the truth of God’s forgiveness and new mercies.

  • Screen adults with the AUDIT or ASSIST and teens with the CRAFFT; all three are free.
