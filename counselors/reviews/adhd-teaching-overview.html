1. What it is

Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is a neurodevelopmental disorder: a lasting pattern of inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity that begins in childhood and interferes with daily life (American Psychiatric Association [APA], 2022). It is not caused by poor parenting, laziness, or weak character.

How common: about 3.4% of children and adolescents worldwide (Polanczyk et al., 2015). In adults, ADHD persisting from childhood affects about 2.6%, and adult ADHD symptoms about 6.8% (Song et al., 2021).

It often continues into adulthood: by age 25, only about 15% still meet full criteria, but about 65% still have impairing symptoms (Faraone et al., 2006).

Counselor framing: ADHD is common, real, and treatable. Medication is the most effective treatment for core symptoms, and counseling helps with skills, family life, and the shame many people carry.

2. Core symptoms to watch for

DSM-5-TR criteria, paraphrased (APA, 2022); use the manual itself for formal diagnosis.

  • Inattention (6 or more symptoms; 5 for age 17 and older): careless mistakes, trouble sustaining attention, not seeming to listen, not finishing tasks, poor organization, avoiding sustained mental effort, losing things, distractibility, forgetfulness.

  • Hyperactivity-impulsivity (6 or more; 5 for age 17 and older): fidgeting, leaving one’s seat, restlessness, difficulty doing things quietly, being "on the go," talking excessively, blurting out answers, trouble waiting, interrupting.

  • Requirements: symptoms for at least 6 months; several present before age 12; present in 2 or more settings (home, school, work); clear interference with functioning; not better explained by another disorder.

  • Presentations: combined, predominantly inattentive, or predominantly hyperactive-impulsive; severity mild, moderate, or severe.

Differential diagnosis (general clinical consensus): anxiety, depression, trauma, sleep problems, learning disorders, substance use, and bipolar disorder can all look like ADHD. Gather history from childhood and from more than one setting.

Screening adults: the 6-question WHO Adult ADHD Self-Report Scale screener outperformed the full 18-question version (sensitivity 68.7%, specificity 99.5%) (Kessler et al., 2005). It is a screen, not a diagnosis.

Risk and safety: across 57 studies, ADHD was linked to suicide attempts (OR 2.37), suicidal thoughts (OR 3.53), and completed suicide (OR 6.69) (Septier et al., 2019). In a national Danish cohort, death rates were about twice expected, mostly from accidents (Dalsgaard et al., 2015).

When counselors must refer (general clinical consensus): refer for diagnostic evaluation and medication consultation, and refer urgently for suicidality or substance misuse.

4. What works: treatment and the evidence

Bottom line for class: medication has the largest effect on core symptoms. Behavioral parent training improves parenting, child conduct, and family life. CBT helps adults who still struggle despite medication.

Medication (referral notes). Across 133 trials, all ADHD medications beat placebo in children; amphetamines (SMD −1.02) and methylphenidate (−0.78) had the largest effects, and the authors named methylphenidate the preferred first choice for children and adolescents and amphetamines for adults (Cortese et al., 2018). In the landmark MTA study, medication management and combined treatment improved core symptoms more than intensive behavioral treatment alone, and combined treatment helped more with conduct, anxiety, social skills, and family relations (MTA Cooperative Group, 1999).

Behavioral and psychological treatment (honest findings):

  • Children: behavioral interventions improved parenting (SMD 0.63 to 0.68), conduct problems, social skills, and academic performance. Their effect on core ADHD symptoms disappeared when raters were blinded to treatment (Daley et al., 2014; Sonuga-Barke et al., 2013).

  • Adults: CBT beat wait-lists by a moderate-to-large margin (SMD 0.76) and active controls by a small-to-moderate margin (SMD 0.43) (Young et al., 2020). In medication-treated adults with remaining symptoms, CBT produced more responders than relaxation and education (53% vs. 23%) (Safren et al., 2010). Evidence quality is still low to moderate (Lopez et al., 2018).

CBT for ADHD: how it is used (Safren et al., 2010):

  • Organization and planning: one calendar and one task list, breaking tasks into steps, and prioritizing.

  • Reducing distractibility: working in short, timed blocks and removing distractions.

  • Challenging unhelpful beliefs that fuel procrastination and shame: "I’m lazy," "I’m stupid," "I’ll fail anyway," "I have to do it perfectly or not at all."

  • For children: parent training in consistent routines, praise, rewards, and calm consequences (Daley et al., 2014).

5. Religion, spirituality, and Christian integration: what the evidence shows

ADHD may make religious life harder. In more than 8,000 young people followed into adulthood, ADHD symptoms predicted lower religious involvement at nearly every stage, and in adulthood some religious practices combined with ADHD to predict worse outcomes (Dew et al., 2022). An earlier pilot study of 117 adolescent outpatients found no link between religiousness and an ADHD diagnosis (Dew et al., 2007).

Faith may still help. Among 578 adults with both substance use disorder and ADHD, higher religiousness was linked to less severe ADHD symptoms, though not through better self-control (Begeman et al., 2025).

Intervention studies. We found no trial of Christian or religiously integrated treatment for ADHD (PubMed search, October 31, 2026). The evidence is limited to the observational studies above.

Honest summary for teaching:

  1. Medication, parent training, and (for adults) CBT are the evidence base. Faith integration supports them; it does not replace them.

  2. ADHD can make prayer, Bible reading, and church attendance feel harder, and people may conclude they are "bad Christians." Faith communities can adapt rather than shame.

  3. No faith-integrated ADHD treatment has been tested. Integrate faith with care and stay alert to guilt and shame.

6. Practical Christian-integration notes

These apply Dr. Campbell’s identify, challenge, replace-with-Scripture method within CBT and family work. Clinical items rest on the research above. Pastoral items are theological application, not research findings.

  • Identify (clinical). Listen for shame-based beliefs: "I’m lazy and undisciplined," "If I really loved God I could focus in prayer," "Taking medication means I don’t trust God," or a parent’s "My child is just rebellious" (Dew et al., 2022). To identify deeper false beliefs, see: Core Beliefs, Parental Messages, or use the Semantic Search Tool.

  • Challenge (clinical). Separate a brain-based difficulty from moral failure, and test the evidence for "lazy." Frame medication and practical supports as wise stewardship, not weak faith (Cortese et al., 2018). Possible cognitive errors to watch for, with their definitions from the Belief Counseling Cognitive Errors Checklist and the symptoms they tend to feed:

  • Global labeling — one mistake becomes who I am. Feeds: shame and low self-esteem. Sounds like: “I’m just lazy.”

  • Overgeneralization — one event becomes a rule. Feeds: discouragement after missed deadlines. Sounds like: “I never finish anything.”

  • Comparisons — measuring myself against others. Feeds: shame. Sounds like: “Everyone else can sit still and get it done.”

  • Should statements — rigid musts and oughts. Feeds: guilt about inattention. Sounds like: “I should be able to focus in prayer like everyone else.”

  • Fortune telling — predicting a bad outcome. Feeds: procrastination and avoiding tasks. Sounds like: “I’ll mess it up, so why start?”

  • Unrealistic expectations — expecting too much of myself or others. Feeds: spiritual guilt about treatment. Sounds like: “If I trusted God, I wouldn’t need medicine.”

  • Jumping to conclusions — deciding without the facts. Feeds: harsh parenting (in parents). Sounds like: “He forgot his homework to defy me.”

  • Questions to dispute the belief. Have the client choose questions from the Questioning False Beliefs form that “speak to” the belief. To see how, watch Dr. Campbell’s video Integrated Christian Counseling: Questioning Your Beliefs. Selected questions that fit the errors above:

  • “Doesn’t everyone have good points and bad points?” For: global labeling.

  • “Am I exaggerating my faults or limitations?” For: overgeneralization.

  • “How have other people handled situations like mine?” For: comparisons.

  • “Do I really have to be perfect? Is any human being perfect?” For: should statements.

  • “Have I dealt with problems like this in the past?” For: fortune telling.

  • “How does the Bible speak to this?” For: unrealistic expectations about medication.

  • “Is it possible that I am distorting the facts?” For: a parent’s jumping to conclusions.

  • Replace (pastoral). Offer Scripture of God’s understanding and grace:

  • “As a father has compassion on his children, so the LORD has compassion on those who fear Him. For He knows our frame; He is mindful that we are dust.” (Psalm 103:13–14)

  • “There are different gifts, but the same Spirit… There are different ways of working, but the same God works all things in all people.” (1 Corinthians 12:4–6)

  • “My grace is sufficient for you, for My power is perfected in weakness.” (2 Corinthians 12:9)

  • “Commit your works to the LORD and your plans will be achieved.” (Proverbs 16:3). Planning tools are a way to commit our work to the Lord.

  • Faith community (clinical). Suggest shorter, active devotional practices (reading plans, audio Bible, written prayer lists), and help children’s ministry leaders use clear routines, movement breaks, and praise.

  • Ministers: avoid treating ADHD behavior as only a spiritual problem; support parents and refer for evaluation.

7. Quick classroom takeaways

  • Diagnose by 6 or more symptoms (5 for adults) in two settings, beginning before age 12. Most children do not "grow out of" all symptoms. Screen adults with the ASRS, and ask about suicide and substances.

  • Medication has the largest effect on core symptoms; parent training helps behavior and family life; CBT helps adults on medication.

  • ADHD can make religious life harder and fuel shame. Replace "lazy" and "bad Christian" with the truth of God’s compassion and grace.

  • Screen adults with the free ASRS and children with the free Vanderbilt scales; gather ratings from more than one setting before diagnosing.
