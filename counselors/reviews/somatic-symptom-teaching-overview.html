1. What it is

In somatic symptom disorder, physical symptoms (pain, fatigue, dizziness, stomach problems) are accompanied by excessive thoughts, worry, and behavior about them. In illness anxiety disorder (formerly hypochondriasis), the person is preoccupied with having or getting a serious illness even though physical symptoms are absent or mild. Both sit in DSM-5-TR’s "Somatic Symptom and Related Disorders" (American Psychiatric Association [APA], 2022).

Key change from older manuals: the symptoms do not have to be medically unexplained. The diagnosis rests on the excessive response, not on whether a doctor can find a cause (APA, 2022).

How common: across 32 primary-care studies (70,085 patients), 26% to 35% met criteria for at least one older somatoform diagnosis, and 40% to 49% reported at least one medically unexplained symptom (Haller et al., 2015).

Counselor framing: the symptoms are real, not "all in the head." Treatment aims at living well and worrying less, not at proving there is nothing wrong. Work alongside the person’s physician.

2. Core symptoms to watch for

DSM-5-TR criteria, paraphrased (APA, 2022); use the manual itself for formal diagnosis.

  • Somatic symptom disorder: one or more distressing or disruptive physical symptoms, plus at least one of: thoughts about their seriousness that are out of proportion; persistently high anxiety about health or symptoms; excessive time and energy devoted to them. Usually lasts more than 6 months. Specifiers: with predominant pain; persistent; mild, moderate, or severe.

  • Illness anxiety disorder: preoccupation with having or getting a serious illness; physical symptoms absent or mild; high health anxiety and easy alarm; excessive checking and doctor visits or avoidance of doctors and hospitals; at least 6 months. Types: care-seeking or care-avoidant.

  • Related diagnoses: functional neurological symptom (conversion) disorder, psychological factors affecting other medical conditions, and factitious disorder (deliberately falsifying symptoms) belong to the same chapter.

Screening and tracking: the PHQ-15 rates physical symptom burden; scores of 5, 10, and 15 mark low, medium, and high severity, and higher scores track worse functioning and more health care use (Kroenke et al., 2002).

Suicide risk: across 33 studies, 24% to 34% of people with these disorders reported current suicidal thinking and 13% to 67% a past attempt, a risk that appeared independent of other mental disorders (Torres et al., 2021). Ask directly.

When counselors must refer (general clinical consensus): make sure a physician has evaluated the symptoms and stays involved; avoid repeated, unnecessary testing. Refer urgently for suicidality, and for new or changing symptoms that need medical review.

4. What works: treatment and the evidence

Bottom line for class: CBT is the best-supported treatment for both disorders, with large effects for illness anxiety and small-to-moderate effects for somatic symptoms. Medication evidence is weak.

Evidence overview (honest effect sizes):

  • Illness (health) anxiety: across 21 comparisons, CBT had a large effect (d = 1.01) that lasted 6 and 12 months (Cooper et al., 2017). In 19 trials, the effect was moderate to large (g = 0.79), 66% responded, 48% remitted, and internet CBT worked about as well as face-to-face (Axelsson & Hedman-Lagerlöf, 2019).

  • In medical clinics: among 444 hospital outpatients with health anxiety, adapted CBT doubled the share reaching normal health-anxiety levels at 1 year (13.9% vs. 7.3%) compared with standard care (Tyrer et al., 2014).

  • Somatic symptoms: across 15 trials (1,671 patients), CBT reduced physical symptoms, anxiety, and depression and improved physical functioning, with lasting benefit, but did not clearly reduce doctor visits (Liu et al., 2019). A Cochrane review of 21 studies found psychological therapies better than usual care, but the effect was small to medium (SMD −0.34) and evidence quality low (van Dessel et al., 2014).

  • Medication (referral notes): in 26 trials, evidence for newer antidepressants and natural products was of low to very low quality, and older tricyclics did not clearly beat placebo (Kleinstäuber et al., 2014).

CBT for these disorders: how it is used (Cooper et al., 2017; Liu et al., 2019):

  • Build a shared, believable explanation in which body, attention, worry, and behavior keep symptoms going.

  • Identify and challenge catastrophic beliefs: "This headache means a brain tumor," "If the doctor didn’t find it, they missed it," "I can’t function while I’m in pain."

  • Reduce safety behaviors such as body checking, internet searching, and repeated reassurance-seeking, and gradually face avoided activities.

  • Rebuild activity and meaning despite symptoms, pacing rather than resting or overdoing.

5. Religion, spirituality, and Christian integration: what the evidence shows

Faith and symptoms: the direction is unclear. In a representative German survey, people with more mental or physical complaints reported more spirituality and private religious practice, which suggests that illness may lead people to seek God as much as faith protects against symptoms (Hampel et al., 2019). Among Dutch cancer patients, spirituality did little to lessen the impact of pain and fatigue on distress, though meaning and acceptance may have helped with fatigue (Visser et al., 2018).

How faith is used matters. Across 49 studies, positive religious coping went with better adjustment to stress and negative religious coping with worse (Ano & Vasconcelles, 2005).

Clinicians’ own beliefs shape care. Among 1,208 U.S. physicians, more religious doctors were more likely to see unexplained symptoms as a spiritual problem (55% vs. 24%) and to think patients would benefit from attending to their spiritual life (Shin et al., 2013).

Intervention studies. We found no trial of Christian or religiously integrated CBT for somatic symptom or illness anxiety disorder (PubMed search, October 31, 2026).

Honest summary for teaching:

  1. CBT, delivered in medical settings or online, is the evidence base. Faith integration supports it; it does not replace it.

  2. Research does not show that faith protects against these disorders; suffering people may turn to faith more. Positive religious coping is still worth supporting.

  3. No faith-integrated trial exists. Avoid telling clients their symptoms are "only spiritual"; that can feel dismissive and delay medical care.

6. Practical Christian-integration notes

These apply Dr. Campbell’s identify, challenge, replace-with-Scripture method within CBT and alongside medical care. Clinical items rest on the research above. Pastoral items are theological application, not research findings.

  • Identify (clinical). Listen for catastrophic health beliefs and for spiritual ones: "God is punishing me with this illness," "If I had enough faith I would be healed," "If I stop checking, something terrible will happen" (Ano & Vasconcelles, 2005). To identify deeper false beliefs, see: Core Beliefs, Parental Messages, or use the Semantic Search Tool.

  • Challenge (clinical). Weigh the evidence for catastrophic predictions, test what happens when checking and reassurance-seeking are reduced, and separate trusting God from demanding certainty about health (Cooper et al., 2017). Possible cognitive errors to watch for, with their definitions from the Belief Counseling Cognitive Errors Checklist and the symptoms they tend to feed:

  • Catastrophizing — expecting the worst possible outcome. Feeds: health anxiety. Sounds like: “This headache must be a brain tumor.”

  • Selective attention — noticing only what fits my belief. Feeds: focus on the body. Sounds like: “I notice every twinge.”

  • Emotional reasoning — I feel it, so it must be true. Feeds: distress about symptoms. Sounds like: “I feel sick, so something must be seriously wrong.”

  • Fortune telling — predicting a bad outcome. Feeds: checking and reassurance-seeking. Sounds like: “If I stop checking, something terrible will happen.”

  • Disqualifying the positive — deciding good things don’t count. Feeds: repeated tests and doctor visits. Sounds like: “The tests were normal, but they missed something.”

  • Personalization — taking blame for things outside my control. Feeds: spiritual distress. Sounds like: “God is punishing me with this illness.”

  • Unrealistic expectations — expecting too much of myself or others. Feeds: spiritual guilt. Sounds like: “If I had enough faith, I would be healed.”

  • Questions to dispute the belief. Have the client choose questions from the Questioning False Beliefs form that “speak to” the belief, then test the answers with exposure. Do not let the questions become a new form of reassurance. To see how, watch Dr. Campbell’s video Integrated Christian Counseling: Questioning Your Beliefs. Selected questions that fit the errors above:

  • “What are the odds that the thing I dread will actually happen?” For: catastrophizing.

  • “Is it possible that I am distorting the facts?” For: selective attention.

  • “What’s the actual evidence for my belief?” For: emotional reasoning.

  • “How often do the things I worry about actually occur?” For: fortune telling.

  • “What are some of the best arguments against my belief or conclusion?” For: disqualifying the positive.

  • “How might an all-loving God look at me or this situation?” For: personalization.

  • “Do I need to “let go and let God” work in my life?” For: unrealistic expectations and demanding certainty.

  • Replace (pastoral). Offer Scripture that steadies worry and honors the body:

  • “My times are in Your hands…” (Psalm 31:15). Trusting God with what cannot be known or controlled.

  • “Therefore do not worry about tomorrow, for tomorrow will worry about itself.” (Matthew 6:34)

  • “Be anxious for nothing, but in everything, by prayer and petition, with thanksgiving, present your requests to God. And the peace of God… will guard your hearts and your minds in Christ Jesus.” (Philippians 4:6–7). Prayer in place of checking.

  • “My flesh and my heart may fail, but God is the strength of my heart and my portion forever.” (Psalm 73:26). Meaning and hope even with ongoing symptoms.

  • Faith community (clinical). Encourage the church to offer practical support without making illness the center of every conversation, and to welcome the person back into service and fellowship as they become more active.

  • Ministers: take physical complaints seriously, encourage one trusted physician, and avoid either dismissing symptoms or praying in ways that heighten fear about them.

7. Quick classroom takeaways

  • Somatic symptom disorder means distressing symptoms plus excessive thoughts, worry, or effort about them; illness anxiety means fear of serious illness with few symptoms. Symptoms need not be medically unexplained. Ask about suicide.

  • CBT has large effects for illness anxiety and smaller effects for somatic symptoms; medication evidence is weak. Coordinate with one physician.

  • Challenge catastrophic and "God is punishing me" beliefs, reduce checking and reassurance, and replace them with trust that our times are in God’s hands.

  • Track physical symptom burden with the PHQ-15 or SSS-8 and health anxiety with the Health Anxiety Inventory.
