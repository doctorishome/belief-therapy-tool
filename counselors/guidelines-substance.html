Belief Counseling · Clinical Practice Guidelines

Substance Use and Addiction

Counseling people who struggle with alcohol and other drugs, and the families who love them

Dr. Brian Campbell

← All Guidelines

These guidelines apply the general Clinical Practice Guidelines to counseling people who struggle with alcohol, prescription drugs, or illegal drugs. Substance problems are common. In 2024, about 48 million Americans aged 12 or older (16.8%) had a substance use disorder in the past year, yet only about 1 in 5 of those who needed treatment received it. Many of these clients will never walk into an addiction program, but they will walk into your office with depression, anxiety, marriage problems, or grief. Trauma and substance use often go together, so the Trauma and PTSD guidelines may apply as well.

Please note: This is a practice framework, not a substitute for professional judgment, supervision, specialized training, the law, licensing-board rules, informed consent, or your profession’s ethics code. Withdrawal and medication decisions belong to medical professionals. Many substance use programs must follow special federal confidentiality rules (42 CFR Part 2); know which rules apply where you work.

Keep these numbers at hand

  • Emergency or suspected overdose: 911
  • 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline: call or text 988
  • SAMHSA National Helpline (free, confidential treatment referral, 24/7, English and Spanish): 1-800-662-4357 (1-800-662-HELP), or text your ZIP code to 435748
  • Find treatment near you: FindTreatment.gov
  • Poison Control: 1-800-222-1222
  • Naloxone (Narcan), which reverses opioid overdose, can be bought without a prescription at most pharmacies.
The central principle
Grace and truth, not shame.

Our goal is to keep clients alive, draw out their own reasons for change, connect them with treatment that works, and help them replace the lies that keep them using with the truth that sets them free.

1Ask Every Client About Substance Use

Identify

Substance problems often hide behind other complaints: depression, anxiety, insomnia, anger, marriage conflict, or trouble at work. Many clients will not bring it up unless you ask, and many Christian clients feel extra shame about drinking or drug use.

In practice

  • Ask every new client about alcohol, prescription medicines, cannabis, and other drugs, in a calm and matter-of-fact way.
  • Use a brief screening tool, such as the AUDIT-C for alcohol or the DAST-10 for drugs, and follow up on any positive answer.
  • Ask what the substance does for the client. People use for reasons: to sleep, to calm down, to numb pain, to feel normal, to fit in.
  • Ask about consequences: health, work, family, money, legal problems, and spiritual life.
  • Use DSM-5-TR criteria to judge whether a substance use disorder is present and how severe it is.
  • Ask again later. Clients often tell more once they trust you.

Try saying

“I ask everyone about alcohol and drugs, because they affect sleep, mood, and relationships. How often do you have a drink?”

“What does it do for you when you use?”

“Let us examine and test our ways, and turn back to the LORD.” (Lamentations 3:40, BSB)

2Put Safety First

Identify

Some substance problems are immediately dangerous. Fentanyl has made overdose far more likely, even for occasional users. Stopping alcohol or sedatives (such as Xanax or Valium) suddenly after heavy use can cause seizures and death. And substance use raises the risk of suicide.

In practice

  • Ask about overdose: past overdoses, using alone, mixing substances, and pills or powders that may contain fentanyl.
  • Encourage clients who use opioids, and their families, to keep naloxone (Narcan) on hand and to learn how to use it.
  • Never advise a client to stop heavy drinking or sedative use “cold turkey.” Refer them to a physician for medically supervised withdrawal.
  • Ask directly about suicidal thoughts, especially when the client is intoxicated, in withdrawal, or after a relapse.
  • Ask about driving while impaired, and about children in the client’s care. Know your duty to report.
  • When someone is unresponsive or not breathing, call 911.

Try saying

“Have you ever overdosed, or been afraid you might? Do you ever use alone?”

“Please don’t stop drinking all at once on your own. With the amount you’ve been drinking, that can be dangerous. Let’s get you to a doctor who can help you stop safely.”

“Rescue those being led away to death, and restrain those stumbling toward the slaughter.” (Proverbs 24:11, BSB)

3Speak About Addiction Without Shame

All steps

Words shape how we treat people. In one study, clinicians who read about a “substance abuser” were more likely to blame and punish him than clinicians who read about the same man described as “having a substance use disorder.” Shame drives many people to hide, lie, and keep using. Scripture speaks honestly about sin, and it also describes a power that enslaves (John 8:34; Romans 7:19). Our clients need both truth and grace.

In practice

  • Use person-first language: “a person with an alcohol use disorder,” not “an alcoholic” or “an addict,” unless the client chooses those words for themselves.
  • Hold responsibility and compassion together. The client is responsible for their choices, and they are also caught in something bigger than willpower alone.
  • Avoid labeling addiction as simply a lack of faith or a lack of trying.
  • Notice your own reactions, especially if addiction has touched your family. Seek supervision when it does.
  • Remind clients that there is no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus (Romans 8:1), and that confession is a step toward freedom, not a sentence.

Try saying

“You are not your addiction. You are a person who is struggling, and there is real help and real hope.”

“For I do not do the good I want to do. Instead, I keep on doing the evil I do not want to do.” (Romans 7:19, BSB)

4Draw Out the Client’s Own Reasons for Change

Challenge

Most people feel two ways about their substance use. Arguing, lecturing, and confronting usually make people defend their use. Motivational interviewing, developed by William Miller and Stephen Rollnick, helps clients voice their own reasons for change, and those reasons carry more weight than ours.

In practice

  • Accept that ambivalence is normal. Explore both sides: what the client likes about using and what worries them.
  • Ask open questions, reflect what you hear, and affirm strengths.
  • Listen for “change talk” (“I don’t want my kids to see me like this”) and invite more of it.
  • Roll with resistance rather than arguing. If you hear “yes, but,” you are pushing too hard.
  • Respect the client’s choice. Harm reduction (using less, never using alone) can be a step toward recovery when the client is not ready to stop.

Try saying

“On a scale of 0 to 10, how important is it to you to make a change? Why that number and not a lower one?”

“What would be different in your life a year from now if you stopped?”

“And a servant of the Lord must not be quarrelsome, but must be kind to everyone, able to teach, and forbearing.” (2 Timothy 2:24, BSB)

5Identify the Beliefs That Fuel Use

Identify · Challenge · Replace

This is where Belief Counseling helps most. Cognitive therapy for addiction, developed by Aaron Beck and colleagues, describes beliefs that drive craving and use: “I can’t cope without it,” “I deserve it after the day I’ve had,” “One won’t hurt,” “I’m not as bad as other people,” and “I’ve already blown it, so I might as well keep going.” Underneath them are often core lies like “I’m worthless,” “I’m alone,” or “God could never forgive this.”

In practice

  • Trace a recent episode of use: the trigger, the thought, the feeling, the decision, and what happened next.
  • Name the permission-giving thoughts that came just before using.
  • Gently examine them with questions. “What happened the last time you told yourself one wouldn’t hurt?”
  • Look for the deeper beliefs about self, others, and God that the substance is numbing.
  • Replace the lies with truth, and practice coping statements for high-risk moments. Belief cards make good homework.

Try saying

“Right before you picked up the bottle, what were you telling yourself?”

“If that thought is a lie, what is the truth?”

“‘Everything is permissible for me,’ but not everything is beneficial. ‘Everything is permissible for me,’ but I will not be mastered by anything.” (1 Corinthians 6:12, BSB)

6Use Proven Treatments and Make Good Referrals

Challenge

Several treatments have strong research support: motivational interviewing, cognitive-behavioral therapy and relapse prevention, contingency management (rewards for drug-free tests, the best-supported treatment for cocaine and methamphetamine), family approaches, mutual-help groups, and medications for alcohol and opioid use disorders. Most clients do best with more than one kind of help.

In practice

  • Match the level of care to the need: outpatient counseling, intensive outpatient, residential treatment, or hospital care. The ASAM Criteria can guide this decision.
  • Know your local resources: addiction medicine doctors, treatment programs, recovery groups, and churches with recovery ministries.
  • Refer when the problem is beyond your training, and keep working with the client when that is appropriate.
  • Coordinate care, with the client’s written permission, so everyone is pulling in the same direction.
  • Treat co-occurring problems such as depression, anxiety, and trauma at the same time, not one after the other.

Try saying

“I want to keep working with you, and I think you’d also benefit from a doctor who specializes in this. Would you be open to that?”

“Plans fail for lack of counsel, but with many advisers they succeed.” (Proverbs 15:22, BSB)

7Support Medication Treatment Without Judgment

Replace

Medications for opioid use disorder (methadone, buprenorphine, and naltrexone) and for alcohol use disorder (such as naltrexone and acamprosate) are effective and underused. A large review found that the death rate for people with opioid dependence was about three times higher out of methadone treatment than in it. Yet some Christians tell clients that medication is “trading one drug for another.”

In practice

  • Learn the basics of these medications so you can answer questions and correct myths.
  • Never pressure a client to stop a prescribed medication. That decision belongs to the client and their physician.
  • Explain that taking a prescribed medication as directed is treatment, not relapse.
  • Warn that the weeks right after stopping opioid treatment, or after detox or jail, carry a high risk of overdose, because tolerance has dropped.
  • Help churches and families understand medication treatment, so the client is not shamed for it.

Try saying

“Taking buprenorphine as your doctor prescribes is treatment, the same way insulin is treatment for diabetes. It’s helping keep you alive while you rebuild your life.”

“Jesus answered, ‘It is not the healthy who need a doctor, but the sick.’” (Luke 5:31, BSB)

8Plan for Recovery, Lapse, and Relapse

Replace

Addiction often behaves like other chronic illnesses. Researchers found that 40 to 60 percent of people treated for alcohol or drug dependence return to use within a year, similar to relapse rates for diabetes, high blood pressure, and asthma. A lapse does not mean treatment failed. But a lapse can turn into a full relapse if the client believes, “I’ve blown it, so why try?”

In practice

  • Build a written relapse prevention plan: triggers, warning signs, coping skills, people to call, and places to avoid.
  • Watch for HALT: Hungry, Angry, Lonely, Tired.
  • Prepare clients for a possible lapse ahead of time, so they know what to do: stop, reach out, and learn from it.
  • After a lapse, respond with curiosity, not disappointment. What led up to it? What can be learned?
  • Celebrate progress: days sober, honest confessions, a craving resisted, a call made.

Try saying

“A slip doesn’t erase the progress you’ve made. Let’s look at what happened, so we can learn from it together.”

“For though a righteous man may fall seven times, he still gets up; but the wicked stumble in bad times.” (Proverbs 24:16, BSB)

9Involve and Support the Family

All steps

Addiction wounds the whole family. Family members often swing between rescuing, nagging, and giving up. Community Reinforcement and Family Training (CRAFT) teaches families to communicate positively, reward sober behavior, let natural consequences happen, and invite their loved one into treatment. In one study, 64% of families trained in CRAFT got a resistant problem drinker into treatment, compared with 30% for a confrontational “intervention” and 13% for Al-Anon–based counseling. Later studies with drug users found similar results.

In practice

  • Offer help to family members even if the person who is using refuses to come.
  • Teach the difference between loving support and enabling, such as paying debts or covering up.
  • Help families set and keep healthy boundaries.
  • Screen for domestic violence and child safety. If there is danger, follow the Abuse guidelines.
  • Point family members to support for themselves, such as Al-Anon, Nar-Anon, or a church family recovery group.
  • Remember the children. They often believe the addiction is their fault.

Try saying

“You can’t make him stop, but there are things you can do that make it more likely he will accept help, and things that will help you stay well.”

“Brothers, if someone is caught in a trespass, you who are spiritual should restore him with a spirit of gentleness. But watch yourself, or you also may be tempted.” (Galatians 6:1, BSB)

10Build a Life and Community of Recovery

Replace

Recovery is more than not using. It means a new way of living: honest relationships, healthy ways to handle stress, purpose, service, and for Christian clients, a growing walk with God. A Cochrane review found that Alcoholics Anonymous and programs that help people take part in it led to higher rates of continuous abstinence than other established treatments.

In practice

  • Encourage mutual-help groups that fit the client: Alcoholics Anonymous, Narcotics Anonymous, Celebrate Recovery, or SMART Recovery.
  • Help the client fill the time and the need the substance once filled: friends, exercise, meaningful work, rest, and worship.
  • Let faith be a resource, not a pressure. Prayer and Scripture help, but they are not a substitute for treatment, and a relapse is not proof of weak faith.
  • Help the client make amends where it is safe and wise.
  • Encourage a recovery-friendly church where the client can be honest and known.

Try saying

“Who are the people who know the real you and can walk with you in this?”

“For if one falls down, his companion can lift him up; but pity the one who falls without another to help him up!” (Ecclesiastes 4:10, BSB)

Common Missteps: What to Say Instead

AvoidTry instead
“You’re an addict, and you need to admit it.”“What do you notice about how alcohol is affecting your life?”
“If you really trusted God, you wouldn’t need this.”“Many faithful people struggle with addiction. God’s grace meets us right where we are.”
“Just stop drinking, starting today.”“Let’s get you to a doctor so you can stop safely.”
“Methadone is just trading one drug for another.”“Your medication is treatment, and it’s helping keep you alive.”
“You relapsed, so we’re starting over from zero.”“You haven’t lost what you’ve learned. Let’s see what happened.”
“You need to kick him out until he hits bottom.”“There are ways to set firm limits and still invite him toward help.”
“Let’s pray, and God will take the craving away.”“Let’s pray, and let’s also make a plan for when the craving comes.”

A Quick Check for Substance Use Counseling

  • Have I asked this client about alcohol, prescription drugs, and other drugs?
  • Is there a risk of overdose, dangerous withdrawal, suicide, or harm to children?
  • Does the client or family have naloxone if opioids are involved?
  • Am I using language that invites honesty rather than shame?
  • Am I drawing out the client’s own reasons for change, or arguing for change myself?
  • Have I identified the beliefs that give permission to use?
  • Is the level of care right, and have I referred for medical or specialized treatment when needed?
  • Do we have a written plan for cravings and for a possible lapse?
  • Is the family getting support too?

For Students

Practice screening questions out loud until they sound natural. Learn the basics of motivational interviewing; it is useful far beyond addiction. Find out what treatment programs, addiction medicine doctors, and recovery groups exist near your practicum site. Bring every case involving overdose risk or withdrawal to supervision, and notice how your own family history with alcohol or drugs affects you.
