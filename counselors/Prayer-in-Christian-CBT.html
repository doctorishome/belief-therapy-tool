For Counselors

Prayer in Christian CBT

What the research shows and how to use it ethically

By Brian Campbell, Ph.D. · Adapted and expanded from “Some Thoughts on Prayer in Christian Counseling” (2015)

“Be anxious for nothing, but in everything, by prayer and petition, with thanksgiving, present your requests to God. And the peace of God, which surpasses all understanding, will guard your hearts and your minds in Christ Jesus.”Philippians 4:6–7 (BSB)

Prayer is the spiritual intervention Christian counselors use most, and the one clients ask about most. Two questions follow. Does research support using prayer in counseling? And how can a counselor use it wisely and ethically?

This article answers both. It reviews what the research shows, then shares the practical guidelines I developed over 25 years at New Life Christian Counseling Center, where most of my clients were Christians and every client knew I was one.

What the Research Shows

Research supports prayer as part of Christian CBT, but evidence for prayer alone as a treatment is still thin and mixed. The studies fall into four groups.

  1. Prayer as part of a Christian CBT packageA 2022 review of religion-adapted CBT trials found that prayer was most often used to support cognitive restructuring, alongside religious content, psychoeducation, and behavioral activation (Costa & Moreira-Almeida, 2022). Pearce and Koenig’s Religiously Integrated CBT (RCBT) is the best-known manualized version, with a free Christian therapist manual (Pearce et al., 2015; Pearce, 2016). Tan (2007) describes the ethical use of prayer, including inner healing prayer, and Scripture within Christian CBT. The limitation: these trials test the whole package, so they cannot show how much of the benefit comes from prayer itself.
  2. Prayer studied directlyIn a small randomized trial, adults with depression or anxiety received six weekly one-hour prayer sessions; their depression and anxiety improved significantly compared with a no-treatment control (Boelens et al., 2009). The gains held at a one-year follow-up of 44 women (Boelens et al., 2012). The sample was small and almost all women, and the control group received no attention at all.
  3. Contemplative prayerKnabb (2012) proposed centering prayer as a Christian alternative to mindfulness-based cognitive therapy for preventing depression relapse, and later co-wrote an eight-week contemplative prayer program for chronic worry (Knabb et al., 2017). Trial results so far are modest: a large online trial (N = 702) found no difference in well-being between centering prayer and comparison conditions (Rohde et al., 2024).
  4. Intercessory prayerThe large STEP study of 1,802 heart-bypass patients found that prayer by strangers had no effect on complications. Patients who were told they were being prayed for had slightly more complications (Benson et al., 2006). The authors stressed that the study did not test whether God exists or answers prayer.

Praying aloud in session has almost no outcome research. What exists is mainly interview studies of a few Christian clients, who generally described the experience as meaningful (England, 2018), and ethics papers urging care (Eriksen & Weld, 2007).

What this means for counselors: use prayer as part of good treatment, not as a stand-alone technique, and never promise a clinical result from it. The practical guidelines below come from clinical experience, not controlled research.

The Counselor’s Own Prayer Life

Prayer in Christian counseling begins with the counselor, before any client arrives. A normal day of counseling will challenge your faith and drain you emotionally and spiritually. Prepare for it through prayer, meditation, and Bible reading. I call this “getting plugged into” God.

From the first client of the day, I want to be in an attitude of prayer, in continuous contact with the Creator and listening for the still small voice of the Holy Spirit. It is like sitting close to your best friend. Try to see each client as God sees them. You alone may not be able to handle every problem you meet, but no problem is too big for the Creator of the universe.

Christian counseling is letting God work through you to bring about change. You are the conduit. Each client should leave having seen God’s love at work in your attitude and character.

In a very real sense, the whole session is like one big prayer.

Silent prayer. I pray silently for clients before and during sessions, and at home when my mind drifts back to them. I also pray silently for wisdom when I feel lost, inept, confused, or frustrated. Silent prayer needs no consent and carries no ethical risk, so it is open to every Christian counselor with every client.

Start with Consent

Praying aloud with a client requires the client’s informed choice. In my practice, every client read and signed a Client Handbook that explained my treatment orientation and offered counseling from either a Christian or a secular perspective.

  • Ask; never assume. A client’s church membership does not mean they want prayer in session.
  • Make it easy to say no, and make clear that saying no will not affect their care.
  • Revisit consent if the client’s wishes change.

This follows the informed-consent model in Is Christian CBT Ethical? Evidence, Insurance, and Informed Consent.

Praying Aloud with Clients

I prayed aloud with about one-third to one-half of the six or seven clients I saw each day. Four practices guided when and how.

Opening prayer: rarely, unless asked
I seldom open a session with prayer. I don’t know what has happened since the last session or what the client’s mood is that day, and prayer at that moment can feel awkward. The exception is when a client asks. Some evangelical Christians want to invite God into the session from the start, and for that minority of clients I begin each session with a brief prayer.
Closing prayer: once trust is clear
After I know a client well and it is clear they would welcome it, I often ask at the end of the session whether it would be okay to pray. By then I am nearly certain the answer is yes. Many clients come to ask for it themselves.
Client-led prayer: welcome, with boundaries
Occasionally a client asks to lead the prayer, and that is usually fine. Watch the length and the motive. One client’s prayers grew so long that I had to end our sessions early to make room for them. Be alert to prayer used, even unknowingly, to control the counselor or the session.
Prayer as summary
A closing prayer can sum up the session’s work and point to what comes next. In belief counseling, it can name the false belief, the truth that replaces it, and the homework ahead. For example:
“Heavenly Father, thank you for the progress we made today in understanding the pain Mary experienced from her mother in childhood. I pray that Mary will soon be able to forgive her mother and move on in her life. Help her identify the lies her mother taught her and replace them with your truths: how much you love her and watch over her. Help her turn to you in her times of pain. Give her time to study the Scriptures this week, and draw her closer to you, the source of all wisdom and truth. Amen.”

This prayer does the work of a CBT session summary: it names the false belief (identify), sets it against God’s truth (challenge), and points to the new belief and the week’s practice (replace). Keep it brief, in the client’s own language, and free of new material the client hasn’t agreed to.

When to Refrain from Praying Aloud

Some situations call for silent prayer only. These guidelines rest on clinical judgment rather than research.

  • Clients who are not Christians, or whose faith is not active. I do not initiate audible prayer with these clients. If one asks for prayer, explore what it would mean to them before deciding.
  • Behavior modification programs. When the work is a clear-cut behavioral plan, such as teaching parents to handle toilet training, temper tantrums, or whining, I rarely introduce prayer.
  • Serious psychological disorders. Use great caution with clients who have psychotic or delusional disorders (such as schizophrenia, schizoaffective disorder, or delusional disorder), dissociative disorders, or severe personality disorders (such as borderline or paranoid personality disorder). These clients may react to prayer negatively or unpredictably, and religious content can become tangled with symptoms. Counselors working with these clients need significant training in the first place.

Prayer Between Sessions

Much of prayer’s value in Christian CBT comes between sessions, as part of the client’s homework. As Christian counselors, we should help clients develop an active prayer life of their own.

  • Daily devotionals. I like books that pair daily Bible reading and prayer for every day of the year. For individual adults, I often recommend Oswald Chambers’ My Utmost for His Highest; for couples, Dennis and Barbara Rainey’s Moments Together for Couples.
  • Written prayers. I have written prayers for people struggling with anxiety and depression. Writing prayers for others is somewhat controversial, but these prayers put into words feelings that are often hard to describe. Clients can use them as a starting point for their own. Two of them appear below.
  • Prayer tied to the belief work. Encourage clients to bring the false belief they are working on to God in prayer, and to pray the truth that replaces it.
  • Learning more. My book Godly Counsel includes a chapter on prayer in Scripture, including the hindrances to prayer.

Two Therapeutic Prayers

These prayers attack anxiety and depression from several directions at once: a thought for the day, a memory verse, a short lesson, and a prayer. Counselors may give them to clients as homework or read them together in session.

Live in the Present

Thought for the Day: Learn to live in the present moment.
Memory Verse: “Do not worry about tomorrow.” (Matthew 6:34)
Challenge: People who are anxious or depressed often spend most of each day thinking about the past or worrying about the future. Abandon your will to God’s will and learn to live in the present.

It’s morning, Lord, and once again I haven’t rested. Even before I open my eyes, my mind has started to wander far back into the shadows of a haunting past, or far ahead into the dread of an imagined future. It seems so hard to control my thoughts—to focus on today, and now.

As I lie here, Lord, awaiting the first light of dawn, I pray that you will still my troubled mind… that you will free me from the intoxicating drugs of fear and sadness that threaten to dull my senses once again… that today will be different… that I will be fully born, and fully awake.

Precious Savior, show me how to live one day at a time. Pull down the curtain on yesterday and tomorrow and teach me the secret of living in day-tight compartments. If my mind starts to drift into the past or the future, gently nudge me back to the here and now. Awaken me to the present moment, and bring me back to my senses.

Open my eyes, and help me to take time to see, really see, the beautiful world around me. Revive my sense of smell, and help me to detect the sweet aromas that lie hidden within each breath I take. Sensitize my skin, and help me to feel and touch the many textures of your precious world. Open my ears, and help me to hear the lovely sounds and songs that play softly behind the loud noise and clamor of life.

My time is short, and I cannot afford the luxury of wasting another day, another precious second. I have spent far too long in the past and the future, and I have often missed the best each moment offered.

As I arise to greet the day, teach me that the real secret of living in the present is to simply make my will, your will. Help me to trust you completely, without hesitation, and without the slightest worry of how things might turn out.

Show me how to abandon myself to you, dear God. Grant me the excitement and the sense of great expectation that comes with living moment by moment in your will—riding on the edge of time and experiencing a glimpse of eternity.

Thy Will Be Done

Thought for the Day: Let God take control of your life.
Memory Verse: “…thy will be done.” (Matthew 6:10)
Lesson: A key step to overcoming psychological problems is to let God guide and direct your life. You need to “let go” and “let God.”

Note for counselors: Near its end, this prayer gives thanks for depression and anxiety, following James 1:2–4 and Romans 5:3–5. A severely depressed client may hear that as being told to feel glad about suffering, or as one more thing they are failing at. Use this prayer once the client has some stability, and talk it through with them first.

Heavenly Father,

I readily acknowledge that I have spent most of my life trying to do things for myself and by myself. I have tried to control my life and steer it in the direction that I felt it should go… the direction that seemed to make perfect logical sense… the direction that seemed so entirely reasonable.

As you know, I have crashed time and time again. And finally, I have reached the point that I can no longer drive.

Heavenly Father, I think I am finally ready to move over and let you take charge… to take a backseat in life… to trust you completely and let you take total control of my destiny.

But I must confess I’m worried. I know my past history. I know that at some point I may be tempted to start driving again… to grab the wheel, to interfere.

As we journey, please protect me from myself. Keep my hands off the steering wheel, and quiet the nagging voices that lure me to take back control…

…Don’t you really think we should be going this way, Lord?

…Can’t you see that it really makes so much sense that this is the direction I should be going?

Precious Savior, help me to realize how dangerous it is to rely upon my own will, my own skill, my own ability to cope with life. Although I have gained some limited success, relief, and happiness, I now realize how perilous and unwise my journey has been.

I cannot fool myself anymore. I can no longer pretend that I know the road… that I know what’s best for me… that I can see what perils lie around the next bend. I have been a fool, and I have teetered on disaster time and time again. I have driven recklessly, with very little thought for Thy will.

Dearest Lord, thank you for my depression and anxiety! I rejoice in my pain and sorrow and in all of the circumstances that have brought me to this point in my life. I have been brought so low I can finally let go.

Over to you, Lord! Thy will be done.

Conclusion

Prayer belongs in Christian CBT when the client chooses it and the counselor uses it wisely. Research supports prayer as part of a Christian CBT package and as a way to strengthen cognitive restructuring, though evidence for prayer alone is still limited. The counselor’s own prayer life comes first; audible prayer with clients follows consent, good timing, and clinical judgment. Above all, prayer reminds both counselor and client that real change comes from God.

Educational note: This article is for counselor education and does not replace clinical training, supervision, or your profession’s ethics code.
