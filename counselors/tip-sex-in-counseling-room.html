Sex in the Counseling Room

Protecting your clients, your license, and your family

Dr. Brian Campbell (2026)

← All Professional Tips
Disclaimer: This tip shares my personal experience and opinions. It is not legal advice. Read the full disclaimer.

A Story I Have Never Forgotten

Let me begin with a story. When I first started counseling, I worked as an independent contractor with a well-established professional Christian counselor. After a couple of years, I suddenly learned that he was being investigated by the licensing board for having sex with one of his clients. I left that practice as quickly as I could.

He was married and had two children. Apparently, he had been having sex with her in his office while I was seeing clients in another office down the hallway.

Clients Who May Test Boundaries

The woman involved was very needy and dependent. That points to something every counselor should know: certain psychological disorders are more likely to bring sexual issues into the counseling room.

Dependent personality disorder. Clients who desperately need someone to lean on can quickly attach to a caring counselor, and the line between support and intimacy can blur.

Histrionic personality disorder. These clients are especially likely to “cross boundaries” and to use sex appeal to manipulate and control the counseling. Three key features listed in the DSM-5-TR (in my own words):

  • Their interactions with others are often inappropriately seductive or sexually provocative.
  • They consistently use their physical appearance to draw attention to themselves.
  • They see relationships as more intimate than they really are.

Borderline personality disorder. Intense, unstable attachments and fear of abandonment can also put pressure on boundaries. I will discuss these personalities at greater length in an upcoming Professional Tip.

Bipolar disorder (manic episodes). During mania, people often lose their normal restraint. At the VA where I did my clinical internship, a group of men with bipolar disorder were known as the “Greyhound Bus Gang.” As summer approached, they would suddenly hop on a Greyhound bus from Pittsburgh to California to have sex with as many college students as they could. (Of course, it is hard to know how much they exaggerated.)

I have seen this in my own office, too. One woman in a manic state nearly drove her car through the front door of my office. Another suddenly stood up during a session and pulled up her blouse to show me the breast implants she had recently gotten.

When a Client Crosses the Line in Session

What should you do when something like that happens? Here is what I did.

I immediately stood up and said:

“I’m sorry, but that is not appropriate in a counseling session. I will need to end our session at this time.”

I opened the door (she had already pulled her blouse back down) and walked down the hallway to the reception area. I told the receptionist that we were ending the session and not to charge for it. Then I turned to the woman, who had followed me, and said:

“I’m sorry that we had to end our session. I will call you and talk with you before we meet again for counseling. There is no charge for today’s session.”

I then went straight to my office and wrote a detailed note in her file describing what had happened.

That documentation is extremely important. By the way, when I called her, she apologized. I later saw her for a few more sessions, and there were no further problems.

If a client crosses a sexual boundary

  1. Stay calm and name the behavior as inappropriate.
  2. End the session, and do not charge for it.
  3. Move to where other people are present.
  4. Document exactly what happened, what you did, and how it was resolved.
  5. Follow up by phone before deciding whether to continue counseling.

Children and Teens

I have also had children and young teens sit in a way that exposed themselves during counseling. With young clients, I calmly and plainly redirect them: “Please keep your legs together while we talk.” Afterward, I have a private word with the parent about appropriate clothing for counseling.

Keep one more thing in mind. Sexualized behavior in a child can be a sign of sexual abuse or exposure to sexual material. Don’t assume it is simply poor manners. Consider whether further assessment is needed, document what you observed, and remember that you are a mandated reporter if you suspect abuse.

Guarding Your Own Heart

We are all human beings, and we are all vulnerable to sexual temptation and the pull of intimacy. As a counselor, you will often need to talk with clients about sexual topics that may make you uncomfortable. At times you may even find yourself fantasizing, or asking more questions than you need to, as someone describes his or her sexual problems or exploits.

If you start to feel sexually aroused by what a client is telling you, quickly move the conversation to a “safer” topic. If the problem continues, run! By that I mean take action, and don’t let yourself be drawn further and further into intimacy:

  • Talk it over with a trusted supervisor or colleague. Secrecy is where trouble grows.
  • Refer the client to a counselor of the client’s own sex, or to a certified sex therapist.
1 Corinthians 6:18 “Flee from sexual immorality. Every other sin a man can commit is outside his body, but he who sins sexually sins against his own body.”

The Consequences Are Severe

Sexual contact with a client is forbidden by every counseling profession’s ethics code, and it almost always means losing your license. In many states it is also a crime. In Georgia, for example, it is a felony, and the client’s consent is no defense.

The ban does not end when counseling ends. Some state laws treat the counseling relationship as lasting “in perpetuity,” that is, forever. The ACA Code of Ethics forbids sexual or romantic relationships with former clients for at least five years after the last professional contact. The APA Ethics Code sets at least two years, and even after that, permits it only in the most unusual circumstances.

Proverbs 7:22–23 “He follows her on impulse, like an ox going to the slaughter, like a deer bounding into a trap, until an arrow pierces his liver, like a bird darting into a snare— not knowing it will cost him his life.”

Sexual intimacy with a client may not cost you your life, but it could certainly cost you your livelihood, your family, and all the years you spent earning your counseling degree. I, for one, worked too hard for my doctorate to throw it away through my own lust or lack of control.

When a Client Flirts With You

What do you do if a client tries to “come on” to you? Fortunately, I did not have much experience with this. I always kept pictures of my wife and children in my counseling room. If I sensed that a woman was being flirtatious or showing too much interest in me, I made a point of talking about my strong, loving relationship with my wife, my love for my children, and my love for God. I rarely had to go further. I think they understood that I was “not going there.”

Practical Safeguards

Dress professionally. If you are a female counselor, dress modestly. Many men are strongly drawn by what they see. No short skirts, sheer blouses, or tops that show cleavage. If you wear a skirt, be careful as you sit down and stand up.

Never counsel alone after hours. Women, never meet with male clients in the evening when no one else is around. The same goes for men. Always make sure at least a secretary or another staff member is in the office while you are counseling.

Be careful with touch. I strongly recommend keeping a professional stance: no hugging clients, touching them on the back, or holding their hands. Once the “hugging process” starts, it can easily be misinterpreted. Clients often caught me off guard by starting a hug. I would politely step back and say:

“Sorry, ____, I’m not permitted to hug clients, even though I might like to. Let’s just shake hands.”

Keep clients sitting up. Should you let clients lie down if you have a couch in your office? I strongly recommend against it. Many people have seen TV portrayals of a client lying on a couch while the therapist sits nearby, a holdover from psychoanalysis. I never felt that this fostered the right atmosphere. I want people sitting up, paying attention, and taking counseling seriously. If a client tries to lie down, simply say:

“I’m sorry, ____, it’s not appropriate to lie down during counseling. Please sit up while we talk.”

Prevention is much better than intervention! Keep your guard up. Don’t be drawn into unnecessary sexual topics or fantasies. Some clients can be very skilled at increasing intimacy with a counselor. Address problems quickly, and don’t linger or hesitate.

Bottom Line

  • Stay on guard with certain clients, especially those with dependent, histrionic, or borderline personality traits, and those in a manic episode.
  • If you begin to feel sexually attracted to a client, stop, talk with a supervisor or trusted colleague, and make a referral.
  • Never meet clients alone with no one else around.
  • Keep touch professional: a handshake, not a hug.
  • If a problem occurs, document what happened, what you did, and how it was resolved.

I hope this helps!
