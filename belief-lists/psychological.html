Each of us has habits of thinking that developed from our experiences, our relationships, and the world around us. This page looks at ten common patterns of belief, such as mistrust, perfectionism, and fear of rejection, and at the false beliefs that often go with them. It is an educational resource, not a test. Recognizing a belief in yourself does not mean something is wrong with you, and it is not a way to label others. These patterns are based on the work of Aaron Beck and other cognitive therapists, written here in plain language. Beliefs overlap, and each person’s story, culture, and faith matter. Our purpose is to notice beliefs that may be inaccurate or too rigid, question them thoughtfully, and replace them with more truthful and constructive ways of thinking.

Use these examples for learning and reflection, not to label yourself or others or replace professional assessment. A preference is not inherently faulty; the target is an inaccurate generalization or rigid rule. Shared religious beliefs are not, by themselves, a sign that something is wrong.

Avoiding Others5 beliefs
  1. “Getting close to people will cost me my freedom.” Open belief card
  2. “I don’t need anyone. I am better off alone.” Open belief card
  3. “Depending on anyone only leads to trouble.” Open belief card
  4. “My feelings aren’t worth sharing. Talking about them changes nothing.” Open belief card
  5. “Relationships take more than they give, so they aren’t worth the effort.” Open belief card
Unusual Thinking and Social Unease5 beliefs
  1. “I am fundamentally unlike other people.”
  2. “Ordinary coincidences carry personal messages.”
  3. “My thoughts can cause unrelated events.”
  4. “Unusual sensations confirm my unusual explanations.”
  5. “Other people’s intentions are usually threatening.”
Rule Breaking5 beliefs
  1. “Exploiting others is necessary for survival.”
  2. “My wants outweigh other people’s rights.”
  3. “Deception is acceptable when it benefits me.”
  4. “Dominating others prevents being dominated.”
  5. “Getting what I deserve justifies harming others.”
Intense Emotions and Fear of Abandonment5 beliefs
  1. “My real self is unacceptable.”
  2. “Important people will eventually leave me.”
  3. “I cannot manage life without someone.”
  4. “Intense feelings will overwhelm me completely.”
  5. “Relationship conflict means rejection is coming.”
Need for Attention and Approval5 beliefs
  1. “Attention determines my worth.”
  2. “I must entertain people to retain affection.”
  3. “Being unnoticed means being insignificant.”
  4. “Stronger displays are necessary to be heard.”
  5. “My feelings reliably establish what is true.”
Specialness and Need for Admiration5 beliefs
  1. “I am entitled to exceptional treatment.”
  2. “Ordinary limits should not apply to me.”
  3. “Admiration establishes my importance.”
  4. “Criticism is an unacceptable humiliation.”
  5. “Other people should prioritize my needs.”
Fear of Rejection and Inadequacy5 beliefs
  1. “Avoiding people and new challenges is safer than risking embarrassment.” Open belief card
  2. “I am inadequate and less than other people.” Open belief card
  3. “I am awkward and unlikable, and I have little to offer.” Open belief card
  4. “I must be sure people will like me before I let them know me.” Open belief card
  5. “Ridicule or criticism would prove I am worthless, and I could not bear it.” Open belief card
Need for Care and Reassurance5 beliefs
  1. “I lack the ability to cope independently.”
  2. “Other people’s judgment is better than mine.”
  3. “Disagreement threatens the support I need.”
  4. “Keeping support requires surrendering my preferences.”
  5. “Being alone means being helpless.”
Perfectionism and Control5 beliefs
  1. “Only flawless performance is acceptable.”
  2. “Mistakes signal personal failure.”
  3. “Strict control prevents disaster.”
  4. “Delegating means accepting unacceptable risks.”
  5. “My standards should govern everyone’s behavior.”
Sources: DSM-5-TR (categories); Beck, Davis, & Freeman (Eds.), Cognitive Therapy of Personality Disorders (3rd ed., 2015), and Beck Institute personality-belief materials (themes). These examples are not a validated assessment or a ranked top-five list. Schizotypal examples are educational syntheses, not an original PBQ scale. OCPD is distinct from OCD.
