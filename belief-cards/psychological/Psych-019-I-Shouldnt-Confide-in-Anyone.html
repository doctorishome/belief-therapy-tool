Psychological/Cognitive False Belief #19 · Social Isolation · Keeping Things Private

“I shouldn’t confide in anyone.”

✦ ✦ ✦

Do you keep your thoughts and feelings to yourself because you do not want to confide in anyone? Does it feel safer to carry everything on your own? The information below is intended to help you take a step toward sharing with someone safe, and to find the relief that comes from knowing God’s word as found in the Bible.

Where it often comes from

A confidence that was betrayed, a childhood where private things were mocked or used against us, or a home where feelings were not welcome can teach us that keeping things to ourselves is the only safe way. Privacy is a healthy value. The belief goes too far when it becomes a rule that no one can be trusted with anything.

How the belief can keep itself going

Not confiding does keep us from being exposed, so the belief seems to work. But the person misses the comfort, perspective, and prayer that come from sharing, and carries everything alone. Because nothing is shared, the belief never meets a trustworthy listener.

What Scripture teaches

Scripture honors wise privacy: “A gossip reveals a secret, but a trustworthy person keeps a confidence.” So safe people exist, and we can seek them. “The intentions of a man’s heart are deep waters, but a man of understanding draws them out.” “Therefore confess your sins to each other and pray for each other so that you may be healed.” And God Himself invites us: “Trust in Him at all times, O people; pour out your hearts before Him. God is our refuge.” We can begin with God, then share a little at a time with someone who has earned trust.

“Trust in Him at all times, O people; pour out your hearts before Him. God is our refuge.” (Psalm 62:8)

Common self-statements

  • It is safer to keep things to myself.
  • Nobody can be trusted with my secrets.
  • Sharing only makes things worse.
  • I should handle my own business.
  • What I say will be used against me.
  • Privacy is the only protection.

Common logical errors

  • All-or-nothing thinking: Believing I must tell everyone everything or tell no one anything.
  • Fortune telling: Predicting that anything I share will be betrayed.
  • Overgeneralizing: Letting one betrayal stand for everyone.
  • Mind reading: Assuming I know how a listener would react.
  • Selective attention: Remembering broken confidences and forgetting kept ones.
  • Emotional reasoning: Feeling exposed and concluding that sharing is dangerous.

Challenging questions

  • What happened that taught me not to confide?
  • Has anyone ever kept something I shared? What happened?
  • What is the difference between healthy privacy and carrying everything alone?
  • What signs show that a person can be trusted?
  • What could I tell God, who already knows everything?
  • What small thing could I share first, and with whom?
  • If a friend told me they could not confide in anyone, what would I want them to know?

Truth summary

Privacy is wise, and so is finding a safe person to confide in. I can start with God, who hears everything, and share a little at a time with someone who has earned trust. I do not have to carry it all alone.

A short prayer

Lord, You know my heart. Teach me to pour it out to You, and to find one trustworthy person to share with. Keep me from carrying everything alone. Amen.

Clinical background

For counselors: beliefs like this appear in Beck and Freeman’s discussion of what clinicians call schizoid personality disorder (Cognitive Therapy of Personality Disorders, 1990, pp. 359–363), where confiding in others is avoided. Many clients have a history of breached confidences, so trust must be earned in session; clear explanations of confidentiality and its limits help. This card follows Identify → Challenge → Replace, using evidence review, balanced alternatives, daily rehearsal, and small behavioral experiments. This is an individualized educational formulation, not a DSM criterion or a diagnostic test. Similar presentations can arise from depression, trauma, social anxiety, autism spectrum differences, or temperament, so careful assessment matters. Pace any experiments to the person; someone with severe depression or thoughts of self-harm should seek professional help.

Try a small behavioral experiment

Choose a safe, low-risk test; do not ignore real warning signs.

  • Write a letter to God telling Him everything on your heart.
  • List the safest people you know and what makes them safe.
  • Share one small, low-risk thing with a safe person, and note how they responded.
  • Ask a trusted person what helps them feel safe confiding in others.
  • Notice a time someone kept your confidence.
  • Write a prayer asking God to bring one trustworthy listener into your life.
