Psychological/Cognitive False Belief #14 · Personality Patterns · Feelings Aren’t Worth Sharing

“My feelings aren’t worth sharing. Talking about them changes nothing.”

✦ ✦ ✦

Personality Patterns · Avoiding Others
An educational example of the habit of keeping feelings to ourselves because sharing them seems pointless, burdensome, or useless. Having this thought does not mean a person has a personality disorder.

Where it often comes from

When feelings were ignored, dismissed, or ridiculed in childhood, a person can learn that they do not matter and that talking about them makes no difference. In some families, feelings simply were not talked about. Over time, silence feels normal and sharing feels pointless.

How the belief can keep itself going

If feelings are never shared, no one has the chance to respond with care, so the person never learns that someone would listen. Unspoken feelings can also fade into numbness, which makes the belief seem true. A single safe experience of being heard can begin to change that.

What Scripture teaches

Scripture invites us to bring our feelings honestly to God. The Psalms are full of complaint, sorrow, and joy poured out before Him. We are also called to carry one another’s burdens and to weep and rejoice together, and that cannot happen unless we share. These passages teach that our feelings matter to God and to the people He puts around us.

“Carry one another’s burdens, and in this way you will fulfill the law of Christ.” (Galatians 6:2)

Common self-statements

  • No one wants to hear how I feel.
  • Talking about it will not help.
  • My feelings are not important.
  • I would just be a burden.
  • It is easier to keep everything inside.
  • Why bother saying it? Nothing will change.

Common logical errors

  • Fortune telling: Predicting that no one will care or understand.
  • Mind reading: Assuming I know how someone will react without asking.
  • Discounting the positive: Ignoring times when sharing helped.
  • All-or-nothing thinking: Believing that if talking cannot fix it, it is useless.
  • Mislabeling: Calling myself a burden instead of a person with needs.
  • Emotional reasoning: Feeling numb and concluding that I have nothing worth saying.

Challenging questions

  • Whose reaction in the past taught me that feelings do not matter?
  • Have I ever shared something and felt better afterward?
  • What does it cost me to keep everything inside?
  • Does talking only matter if it fixes the problem, or can being heard help by itself?
  • What would God want to hear from me?
  • Who is one safe person I could tell one small feeling?
  • If a friend carried what I carry, would I want them to tell me?

Truth summary

My feelings matter to God, and they can matter to the right people. Sharing does not always fix a problem, but being heard can lighten it. I can start small and choose safe people. God invites me to bring everything to Him, and He cares.

A short prayer

Lord, You hear every feeling I pour out. Help me bring them to You, and give me safe people who will listen. Teach me that I am not a burden, and that my heart matters to You. Amen.

Clinical background

For counselors: beliefs like this are often discussed in connection with what clinicians call schizoid personality disorder, a long-standing pattern of detachment from close relationships and a narrow range of expressed emotion. This card follows Identify → Challenge → Replace, using evidence review, balanced alternatives, daily rehearsal, and small behavioral experiments. Limited emotional expression may reflect temperament, learned family patterns, depression, or trauma; helping a person name even one feeling to one safe listener is a workable first step. This is an individualized educational formulation, not a DSM criterion or a diagnostic test. Similar presentations can arise from depression, trauma, social anxiety, autism spectrum differences, or temperament, so careful assessment matters. Pace any experiments to the person; someone with severe depression or thoughts of self-harm should seek professional help.

Try a small behavioral experiment

Choose a safe, low-risk test; do not ignore real warning signs.

  • Write down one feeling each day for a week, even in a few words.
  • Tell God one honest feeling in prayer, using Psalm 142:2 as a guide.
  • Share one small feeling with a trusted person, and note how they responded.
  • Ask a safe person, “Can I tell you something that has been on my mind?”
  • Notice a time this week when you felt something and chose not to say it, and write what you feared.
  • Choose a feelings word list and circle three words that fit your week.
