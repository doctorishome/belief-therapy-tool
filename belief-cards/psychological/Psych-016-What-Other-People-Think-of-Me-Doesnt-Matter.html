Psychological/Cognitive False Belief #16 · Social Isolation · Opinions Don’t Matter

“What other people think of me doesn’t matter.”

✦ ✦ ✦

Do you tell yourself that other people’s opinions of you simply do not matter? Do praise and criticism both seem to slide right off you? The information below is intended to help you take a step toward valuing the honest voices of people who care about you, and to find the wisdom that comes from knowing God’s word as found in the Bible.

Where it often comes from

Some people learn early that other people’s opinions were unreliable, harsh, or impossible to please, so they decide to stop caring. Others have been hurt so often by criticism that indifference feels safer than hope. There is a healthy freedom from the fear of what people think. The belief goes too far when it shuts out the caring feedback and encouragement that make us wiser.

How the belief can keep itself going

Not caring what anyone thinks feels strong and protects against hurt. But it also means the person stops hearing honest feedback, kind words, and gentle correction from those who truly care. Without those voices, nothing challenges the belief, and distance can grow without anyone noticing.

What Scripture teaches

Scripture frees us from the fear of man: “The fear of man is a snare, but whoever trusts in the LORD is set securely on high.” But it does not tell us to stop listening to people altogether. “The way of a fool is right in his own eyes, but a wise man listens to counsel.” “The wounds of a friend are faithful.” “As iron sharpens iron, so one man sharpens another.” We do not live for people’s approval, but we are wiser when we receive the counsel of those who love us.

Common self-statements

  • I do not care what anyone thinks.
  • Praise and criticism mean nothing to me.
  • Their opinions are irrelevant.
  • I set my own standards.
  • No one’s view of me affects me.
  • I do not need anyone’s approval or advice.

Common logical errors

  • All-or-nothing thinking: Seeing the choice as either caring about everyone’s opinion or no one’s.
  • Mislabeling: Calling hurt “indifference.”
  • Discounting the positive: Ignoring kind words and helpful feedback.
  • Overgeneralizing: Turning a few unreliable critics into “no one’s opinion counts.”
  • Selective attention: Remembering the criticism and forgetting the encouragement.
  • Emotional reasoning: Feeling unaffected and concluding that I really do not care.

Challenging questions

  • Is it true that I do not care at all, or did I learn not to show it?
  • Whose opinion did I once care about, and what happened?
  • Is there anyone whose honest view I could trust?
  • What might I be missing by not hearing what caring people think?
  • What is the difference between living for approval and being open to counsel?
  • What does Scripture say about listening to wise people?
  • If a friend told me their opinion of me did not matter, what would I want them to know?

Truth summary

I do not have to live for anyone’s approval, and I do not have to be ruled by criticism. But the honest voices of people who care about me are a gift. I can stay free of the fear of man and still listen to wise counsel and kind words.

A short prayer

Lord, I stopped caring what people think because it hurt too much. Set me free from the fear of man, and help me receive the wisdom and kindness of people who love me. Amen.

Clinical background

For counselors: beliefs like this appear in Beck and Freeman’s discussion of what clinicians call schizoid personality disorder (Cognitive Therapy of Personality Disorders, 1990, pp. 359–363), where indifference to praise and criticism is one feature of a long-standing pattern of detachment from close relationships. A healthy independence from other people’s opinions is not a problem; the concern is a rigid conviction that no one’s view matters, especially when it narrows life or costs the person support. This card follows Identify → Challenge → Replace, using evidence review, balanced alternatives, daily rehearsal, and small behavioral experiments. This is an individualized educational formulation, not a DSM criterion or a diagnostic test. Similar presentations can arise from depression, trauma, social anxiety, autism spectrum differences, or temperament, so careful assessment matters. Pace any experiments to the person; someone with severe depression or thoughts of self-harm should seek professional help.

Try a small behavioral experiment

Choose a safe, low-risk test; do not ignore real warning signs.

  • List three times someone’s kind or honest words helped you, and note how it felt.
  • Ask one trusted person for their honest view on something small, and record what happened.
  • Accept a compliment with a simple “thank you,” and notice your reaction.
  • Tell someone one thing you appreciate about them.
  • Notice a time this week when you cared more than you expected.
  • Write a prayer asking God to help you receive the good words of others.
