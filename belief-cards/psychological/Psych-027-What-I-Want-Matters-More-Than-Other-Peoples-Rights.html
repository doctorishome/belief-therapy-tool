Psychological/Cognitive False Belief #27 · Honesty and Respect · Wants and Fairness

“What I want matters more than other people’s rights.”

✦ ✦ ✦

Do rules and other people’s needs sometimes feel like obstacles to what you want? Have you found it hard to see why you should wait or give way? The information below is intended to help you take a step toward respect that lasts, and to find the freedom that comes from knowing God’s word as found in the Bible.

Where it often comes from

A child who was indulged without limits, or who learned that taking was the only way to get anything, can grow up treating wants as rights. Others learned this in homes where the strongest got their way. Either way, the habit says my desires come first.

How the belief can keep itself going

Taking what I want brings quick satisfaction, so the habit is rewarded. But people get hurt or angry, and I may shrug off their complaints as weakness or unfairness, which leaves me the one wronged. The cost to relationships and trust rarely feels tied to my own choices.

What Scripture teaches

Scripture asks us to see other people as real. “Do nothing out of selfish ambition or empty pride, but in humility consider others more important than yourselves. Each of you should look not only to your own interests, but also to the interests of others.” “Do to others as you would have them do to you.” And even in freedom, “do not use your freedom as an opportunity for the flesh. Rather, serve one another in love.” Respect for others is not weakness; it is the way to a good life.

“Do nothing out of selfish ambition or empty pride, but in humility consider others more important than yourselves. Each of you should look not only to your own interests, but also to the interests of others.” (Philippians 2:3–4)

Common self-statements

  • I deserve what I want.
  • Why should I wait?
  • Their problem is not mine.
  • Rules are for other people.
  • If I want it, I should have it.
  • Nobody should stand in my way.

Common logical errors

  • Entitlement: Assuming my wants outrank other people’s needs.
  • Emotional reasoning: Wanting something strongly and concluding I should have it.
  • Minimizing: Treating harm to others as small or deserved.
  • Personalization of blame: Seeing myself as wronged whenever I do not get my way.
  • All-or-nothing thinking: Believing I either get my way or I lose.
  • Short-term thinking: Counting the gain now and not the cost later.

Challenging questions

  • Who is affected when I put what I want first?
  • How would I feel if someone treated me this way?
  • What have I lost because of this approach?
  • Is wanting something strongly the same as having a right to it?
  • What would it look like to wait, share, or give way once?
  • What does Scripture say about considering others?
  • If someone I love lived this way, what would I hope for them?

Truth summary

My wants matter, but so do other people’s rights. Respect and fairness bring trust and lasting good. I can want things, wait for them, and still treat people well, and that kind of life is stronger than getting my way.

A short prayer

Lord, thank You that I matter and that others matter too. Soften my heart, teach me to consider those around me, and lead me into a life of respect and fairness. Amen.

Clinical background

For counselors: this belief appears in the cognitive model of what clinicians call antisocial personality disorder (Beck, Davis, & Freeman, Cognitive Therapy of Personality Disorders), where views such as “I have to look out for myself,” “others are weak and exploitable,” and “rules do not apply to me” are central. Entitlement and low empathy for others’ interests are often paired with a belief that one’s own needs are urgent; explore the costs, practice perspective-taking, and use the person’s own goals to motivate. Expect little spontaneous distress about these beliefs; engagement usually begins with the person’s own goals and costs (relationships, legal trouble, loss of trust), so use collaborative questioning rather than confrontation, set clear boundaries, and keep sessions practical. Assess risk of harm to others, abuse or violence in the home, substance use, and suicide risk; consult or refer as indicated (in the U.S., 988 for crisis). This is an individualized educational formulation, not a DSM criterion or a diagnostic test. Similar presentations can arise from trauma, a hard upbringing, substance use, or other conditions, so careful assessment matters.
