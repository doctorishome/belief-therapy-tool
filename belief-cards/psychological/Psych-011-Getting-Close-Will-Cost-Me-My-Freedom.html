Psychological/Cognitive False Belief #11 · Closeness and Connection · Closeness Costs Freedom

“Getting close to people will cost me my freedom.”

✦ ✦ ✦

Do you find yourself pulling away when someone tries to get close? Do you worry that letting people in will mean losing your independence or being controlled? The information below is intended to help you take a step toward seeing that closeness with safe people does not have to cost you your freedom, and to find the peace that comes from knowing God’s word as found in the Bible.

Where it often comes from

A person who grew up with controlling, intrusive, or unpredictable caregivers may learn that being close means being managed, criticized, or swallowed up. Others learned that needs were a burden and that self-reliance was the safe way to live. Distance then feels like freedom, even when it has quietly become the walls of a small life.

How the belief can keep itself going

Keeping people at arm’s length does protect us from being controlled, so it seems to work. But the same distance blocks the experience of being close to someone who respects us, so the belief never gets tested. We also tend to picture closeness at its worst. Gathering evidence from safe, healthy relationships can start to loosen it.

What Scripture teaches

Scripture does not describe freedom as the absence of people. Christ sets us free, and that freedom is meant to be lived out in love for others. God places the lonely in families. Real love does not seize control; it serves. These passages teach that closeness and freedom were never meant to be enemies.

“For you, brothers, were called to freedom; but do not use your freedom as an opportunity for the flesh. Rather, serve one another in love.” (Galatians 5:13)

Common self-statements

  • If I let someone in, they will take over my life.
  • I need my space, and people always want more of me.
  • Being close means giving up who I am.
  • I stay free by not getting involved.
  • Commitments are cages.
  • It is easier to keep everyone at a distance.

Common logical errors

  • All-or-nothing thinking: Believing I am either completely free and alone or completely controlled and close.
  • Fortune telling: Predicting that anyone close to me will take over.
  • Overgeneralizing: Assuming everyone will treat me the way a controlling person once did.
  • Emotional reasoning: Taking the feeling of being crowded as proof that I am in danger.
  • Discounting the positive: Dismissing times when someone was close and still respected my space.
  • Mislabeling: Calling every kind of closeness “control.”

Challenging questions

  • Who, in my past, made closeness feel like a loss of freedom, and is everyone like that person?
  • Can I think of a person who respected my space and my choices?
  • What has staying distant cost me, and what has it protected?
  • Can closeness have boundaries, so that I can say yes or no and still be close?
  • What does Scripture say real freedom is for?
  • Could a small, chosen amount of closeness feel different from being controlled?
  • What is one safe person with whom I could practice saying what I need?

Truth summary

Closeness and freedom are not opposites. Healthy relationships have room for boundaries, differences, and choices. I can say no and still be loved. Christ gives me true freedom, and He invites me to use it to love and be loved. I can choose how close to get, one safe step at a time.

A short prayer

Lord, You know why I guard my freedom so tightly. Heal the places where closeness once hurt. Show me safe people, teach me healthy boundaries, and help me use the freedom You gave me to love and be loved. Amen.

Clinical background

For counselors: beliefs like this are often discussed in connection with what clinicians call schizoid personality disorder, a long-standing pattern of detachment from close relationships and a narrow range of expressed emotion. This card follows Identify → Challenge → Replace, using evidence review, balanced alternatives, daily rehearsal, and small behavioral experiments. Detachment is sometimes a learned response to intrusive or controlling relationships; distinguishing a person’s real boundary needs from fear-based avoidance helps set the pace. This is an individualized educational formulation, not a DSM criterion or a diagnostic test. Similar presentations can arise from depression, trauma, social anxiety, autism spectrum differences, or temperament, so careful assessment matters. Pace any experiments to the person; someone with severe depression or thoughts of self-harm should seek professional help.

Try a small behavioral experiment

Choose a safe, low-risk test; do not ignore real warning signs.

  • Choose one safe person and spend a short, planned time together, such as a coffee or a walk. Decide in advance how long you will stay.
  • Practice one respectful “no” or “not right now” with someone safe, and notice whether the relationship survives.
  • Tell a trusted person one small thing you need, such as quiet time after a gathering.
  • List three ways a close relationship could add to your freedom, such as help, company, and encouragement.
  • Notice one moment this week when someone respected your space, and write down what happened.
  • Write a prayer about what you fear losing, and read Galatians 5:13 aloud.
