Psychological/Cognitive False Belief #30 · Honesty and Respect · Fairness and Payback

“If I’ve been wronged, I have the right to get even.”

✦ ✦ ✦

Have you been hurt or treated unfairly, and found it hard to let it go without getting even? Does it seem only fair that people should pay for what they have done? The information below is intended to help you take a step toward peace, and to find the freedom that comes from knowing God’s word as found in the Bible.

Where it often comes from

Many who think this way have really been wronged, sometimes badly, and nobody made it right. When justice never came, taking matters into your own hands can seem the only fair response. The anger underneath is understandable.

How the belief can keep itself going

Getting even tends to start the next round: the other person retaliates or distances, and now I have a new wrong to answer. Holding on to a grievance also keeps the hurt alive in me. The person I most hurt is often myself.

What Scripture teaches

Scripture takes real wrongs seriously and still points away from revenge. “Do not repay anyone evil for evil. Carefully consider what is right in the eyes of everybody.” “Wait on the LORD, and He will save you.” “Do not be overcome by evil, but overcome evil with good.” “Do not be deceived: God is not to be mocked. Whatever a man sows, he will reap in return.” God sees what was done to us and will deal with it justly.

Common self-statements

  • They had it coming.
  • I will not let this go.
  • Somebody has to pay.
  • If I do not hit back, I look weak.
  • It is only fair.
  • No one will protect me but me.

Common logical errors

  • Emotional reasoning: Feeling angry and concluding payback is justified.
  • All-or-nothing thinking: Believing I either strike back or I am a doormat.
  • Magnifying: Seeing the wrong as bigger than any other consideration.
  • Fortune telling: Predicting that unless I act, no one will.
  • Overgeneralizing: Treating one wrong as a reason to be hard on everyone.
  • Short-term thinking: Counting the satisfaction and not the consequences.

Challenging questions

  • What happened to me, and was it dealt with?
  • What would getting even actually accomplish?
  • What has it cost me when I have retaliated before?
  • Is there a way to get justice without revenge?
  • What does it do to me to keep carrying this?
  • What does Scripture say about God’s justice?
  • What would I advise a friend in my place?

Truth summary

I may have really been wronged, and my anger is understandable. But getting even rarely heals the hurt and often adds new trouble. I can seek justice in healthy ways, trust God to deal with what I cannot, and free myself from carrying the wrong.

A short prayer

Lord, You see what was done to me. Help me to release the need for payback, to seek what is right, and to trust You with what I cannot fix. Amen.

Clinical background

For counselors: this belief appears in the cognitive model of what clinicians call antisocial personality disorder (Beck, Davis, & Freeman, Cognitive Therapy of Personality Disorders), where views such as “I have to look out for myself,” “others are weak and exploitable,” and “rules do not apply to me” are central. Retaliatory attitudes often rest on genuine mistreatment; validate the injury, separate justice from revenge, and use the person’s own costs (legal trouble, lost relationships) as motivation. Assess for intent to harm specific people and respond according to duty-to-warn standards. Expect little spontaneous distress about these beliefs; engagement usually begins with the person’s own goals and costs (relationships, legal trouble, loss of trust), so use collaborative questioning rather than confrontation, set clear boundaries, and keep sessions practical. Assess risk of harm to others, abuse or violence in the home, substance use, and suicide risk; consult or refer as indicated (in the U.S., 988 for crisis). This is an individualized educational formulation, not a DSM criterion or a diagnostic test. Similar presentations can arise from trauma, a hard upbringing, substance use, or other conditions, so careful assessment matters.

Try a small behavioral experiment

Choose a safe, low-risk test; do not ignore real warning signs.

  • Write down what happened and what you wish had happened.
  • Before acting on anger, wait a day and write the possible outcomes.
  • Tell a safe person about the wrong instead of acting on it.
  • List the costs of past payback.
  • Pray Romans 12:17 when you feel the urge to retaliate.
  • Do one small good thing for someone, and note what it does for you.
