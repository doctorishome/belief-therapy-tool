Psychological/Cognitive False Belief #29 · Honesty and Respect · Power and Control

“Staying in control keeps me from being controlled.”

✦ ✦ ✦

Do you feel you have to be the one in charge, or someone else will have power over you? Does giving way feel like losing? The information below is intended to help you take a step toward strength that does not need to dominate, and to find the security that comes from knowing God’s word as found in the Bible.

Where it often comes from

Many people who hold this belief were once controlled themselves by a harsh parent, an abusive partner, or a rough environment. The lesson they took was never to be the weak one again. The need to stay on top then becomes a way of staying safe.

How the belief can keep itself going

Pushing, intimidating, or winning at all costs can work in the moment, and it leaves others afraid, angry, or distant. Every relationship becomes a contest, and I may feel I can never relax. The strongest bonds, built on trust, never get the chance to form.

What Scripture teaches

Jesus showed another kind of strength. “You know that those regarded as rulers of the Gentiles lord it over them… But it shall not be this way among you.” And “The Son of Man did not come to be served, but to serve.” We are told to be “not lording it over those entrusted to you, but being examples to the flock.” “Pride goes before destruction, and a haughty spirit before a fall.” Real security comes from God, not from dominating others.

“You know that those regarded as rulers of the Gentiles lord it over them, and their superiors exercise authority over them. But it shall not be this way among you.” (Mark 10:42–43)

Common self-statements

  • If I do not take charge, someone else will.
  • I will never be pushed around again.
  • Respect means fear.
  • Giving in is weakness.
  • I have to win.
  • People only listen to force.

Common logical errors

  • All-or-nothing thinking: Seeing every relationship as either dominating or being dominated.
  • Overgeneralizing: Treating everyone as a threat because someone once hurt me.
  • Fortune telling: Predicting that any softness will be exploited.
  • Emotional reasoning: Feeling threatened and concluding I must dominate.
  • Mistaking fear for respect: Assuming people who fear me respect me.
  • Short-term thinking: Counting the win and not the damage to relationships.

Challenging questions

  • When did I first feel controlled or powerless?
  • What do I actually fear would happen if I gave way?
  • Is it true that everyone is trying to control me?
  • What has dominating cost me in relationships?
  • Could I be strong and still gentle?
  • What does Scripture say about true greatness?
  • What kind of person do I want to be?

Truth summary

I can be strong without dominating. Real security comes from God, not from controlling people. I can give way, listen, and cooperate without being weak, and gentle strength builds respect that lasts.

A short prayer

Lord, You know what taught me to fear being controlled. Give me security in You, and teach me a strength that serves instead of dominates. Amen.

Clinical background

For counselors: this belief appears in the cognitive model of what clinicians call antisocial personality disorder (Beck, Davis, & Freeman, Cognitive Therapy of Personality Disorders), where views such as “I have to look out for myself,” “others are weak and exploitable,” and “rules do not apply to me” are central. A history of being controlled or abused often underlies dominance; address the old injury, reframe strength, and rehearse assertive but non-intimidating behavior. Assess for ongoing violence or intimidation in the home. Expect little spontaneous distress about these beliefs; engagement usually begins with the person’s own goals and costs (relationships, legal trouble, loss of trust), so use collaborative questioning rather than confrontation, set clear boundaries, and keep sessions practical. Assess risk of harm to others, abuse or violence in the home, substance use, and suicide risk; consult or refer as indicated (in the U.S., 988 for crisis). This is an individualized educational formulation, not a DSM criterion or a diagnostic test. Similar presentations can arise from trauma, a hard upbringing, substance use, or other conditions, so careful assessment matters.
