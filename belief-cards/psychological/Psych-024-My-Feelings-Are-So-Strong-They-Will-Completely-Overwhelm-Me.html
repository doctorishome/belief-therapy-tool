Psychological/Cognitive False Belief #24 · Closeness and Steadiness · Strong Feelings

“My feelings are so strong they will completely overwhelm me.”

✦ ✦ ✦

Do your feelings sometimes surge so quickly and strongly that it seems you cannot cope? Do you wonder where the calm went? The information below is intended to help you take a step toward steadier feelings, and to find the peace that comes from knowing God’s word as found in the Bible. If strong feelings ever lead to thoughts of self-harm, seek help right away (in the U.S., call or text 988).

Where it often comes from

Some people are born more emotionally sensitive, and others grew up where feelings were ignored, punished, or unpredictable, so they never learned how to settle them. When emotions feel enormous and nobody showed a way through, it is natural to fear them.

How the belief can keep itself going

Fear of the feeling adds another layer on top of it. Pushing feelings away, or acting fast to end the pain, can bring short relief but teaches that the feeling must be escaped. Feelings that are avoided often return larger. Without practice in riding them out, the fear stays.

What Scripture teaches

Scripture is honest about strong feelings and about God’s help in them. “When anxiety overwhelms me, Your consolation delights my soul.” We are invited to “Cast all your anxiety on Him, because He cares for you.” “Be anxious for nothing, but in everything, by prayer and petition, with thanksgiving, present your requests to God. And the peace of God, which surpasses all understanding, will guard your hearts and your minds in Christ Jesus.” And God Himself is compassionate: “He knows our frame; He is mindful that we are dust.” Feelings rise and fall; they are not the whole truth about me.

Common self-statements

  • I cannot stand this feeling.
  • It is too much.
  • I am going to fall apart.
  • I will lose control.
  • This will never stop.
  • I have to do something right now.

Common logical errors

  • Emotional reasoning: Feeling overwhelmed and concluding I am being destroyed.
  • Catastrophizing: Imagining the feeling will never end or harm me.
  • All-or-nothing thinking: Believing I either control the feeling or it controls me.
  • Fortune telling: Predicting that I will lose control.
  • Overgeneralizing: Turning one bad moment into “I always fall apart.”
  • Discounting the positive: Forgetting the times the feeling passed.

Challenging questions

  • How long have my strong feelings lasted in the past?
  • What happened after the peak, when the feeling eased?
  • What do I actually fear will happen if I feel this?
  • Have I ever gotten through a surge of emotion before?
  • What helps my body settle (breathing, walking, praying)?
  • What does Scripture say about God’s help in my distress?
  • If a friend felt this way, what would I say to them?

Truth summary

Strong feelings are painful, but they rise, peak, and pass. They do not destroy me. I can breathe, pray, wait, and ask for help, and I can learn skills to steady myself. God is near, and His peace can guard my heart.

A short prayer

Lord, You see how strong my feelings can be. Draw near to me, quiet my heart, and teach me to calm myself and to trust You through each wave. Amen.

Clinical background

For counselors: this belief appears in the cognitive model of what clinicians call borderline personality disorder (Beck, Davis, & Freeman, Cognitive Therapy of Personality Disorders), where fear of abandonment, all-or-nothing thinking about people, an unsettled sense of self, and a fear of being overwhelmed by emotion are central. Emotional dysregulation and fear of intense affect are central; teach distress tolerance (breathing, grounding, delaying urges), emotion labeling, and gradual exposure to feelings. Assess impulsive or self-harm behaviors used to escape emotion, and make a safety plan when indicated. Behavioral skills for tolerating distress and regulating emotion (as in Linehan’s DBT) pair well with this card’s Identify → Challenge → Replace work. Screen for self-harm and suicide risk, and consult or refer when risk is present (in the U.S., 988). This is an individualized educational formulation, not a DSM criterion or a diagnostic test. Similar presentations can arise from trauma, depression, anxiety, or temperament, so careful assessment matters. Pace any experiments to the person.

Try a small behavioral experiment

Choose a safe, low-risk test; do not ignore real warning signs.

  • When a strong feeling comes, name it and rate it from 0 to 10, then rate it again after ten minutes.
  • Practice slow breathing for two minutes (in for four, out for six).
  • Wait ten minutes before acting on an urge, and pray Philippians 4:6–7.
  • Tell a safe person what you are feeling instead of acting on it.
  • Take a short walk when a feeling surges.
  • Write what helped after a hard moment passed.
