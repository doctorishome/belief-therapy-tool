Psychological/Cognitive False Belief #1 · Personality Patterns · Mistrust

“Relaxing my guard leaves me defensless.”

✦ ✦ ✦

Personality Patterns · Mistrust and Suspicion
An educational example of a mistrust belief. Recognizing this belief does not establish a diagnosis. Similar beliefs can occur in many contexts.

Where it often comes from

Being betrayed, bullied, deceived, or mistreated can teach a person to stay alert. Unpredictable relationships, repeated criticism, or messages that nobody can be trusted may strengthen this expectation. These are possible learning experiences, not an explanation for every person. A protective rule can become too broad when it is applied to everyone, even when the present evidence is different.

How the belief can keep itself going

Expecting harm can lead to scanning for threats, withdrawing, testing people, or responding defensively. Those responses may reduce opportunities to learn that some relationships are dependable. The task is to compare expectations with present evidence while preserving appropriate boundaries.

What Scripture teaches

God invites us to put our trust in Him when we are afraid, rather than letting fear determine every relationship. Scripture also describes the goodness of companionship, receiving help, carrying one another’s burdens, and loving each other. These passages challenge the expectation that closeness can bring only harm. With God’s help, I can make room for people who show care and reliability, accept support, and discover that I do not have to face life alone.

Trust in the LORD with all your heart, and lean not on your own understanding; in all your ways acknowledge Him, and He will make your paths straight (Proverbs 3:5–6).

Common self-statements

  • People always have a hidden agenda.
  • If someone is kind, they probably want something.
  • I must watch for every sign of betrayal.
  • If I relax, I will miss the danger.
  • I cannot tell anyone what I really think.
  • Depending on someone gives them power to hurt me.

Common logical errors

  • Overgeneralization: Applying one person’s betrayal to all people.
  • Mind reading: Treating a guess about someone’s motives as a known fact.
  • Selective attention: Noticing signs of danger while overlooking consistent, respectful behavior.
  • Emotional reasoning: Taking the feeling of being unsafe as proof of a present threat.
  • All-or-nothing thinking: Assuming my only choices are complete trust or complete distrust.
  • Catastrophizing: Predicting serious harm from an ordinary interaction without enough evidence.

Challenging questions

  • What happened that I can directly observe, and what meaning have I added?
  • What evidence supports my concern? What evidence points another way?
  • Am I responding to this person’s behavior or to an earlier betrayal?
  • What are two other possible explanations?
  • Has this person respected a clear boundary before?
  • What small amount of trust, if any, fits the evidence?
  • Can I protect myself without assuming that everyone intends harm?

Truth summary

I can trust God with my fears and allow caring people to become part of my life. Past betrayal does not determine what every new relationship will be. Some people are dependable, and I can learn to recognize and receive their care. I can take small steps toward connection, ask for help, and let trust grow through shared experience.

Clinical background

For counselors: beliefs like this are often discussed in connection with what clinicians call paranoid personality disorder. This original educational example uses cognitive restructuring, checking interpretations, and modest behavioral experiments. It is informed by the cognitive model of personality beliefs; it is not a DSM criterion, a quotation from Beck, or a validated treatment protocol.

Beck Institute: Beliefs in personality disorders · Beck, Davis, & Freeman (Eds.), Cognitive Therapy of Personality Disorders (3rd ed.)

My practice plan

Everyone does these
  • Make my Truth Card (below). Carry it and read it at least three times a day.
  • Memorize my Scripture from Step 4.
  • Listen to one song each day from “Songs that speak truth to this belief” on my card, and fill in my song journal (below).
  • Set reminders to silently repeat my truth. Check one or more: ☐ A buzz: my watch or phone vibrates every ______ minutes
    ☐ An everyday event: every time I ______________________________
    ☐ A visual reminder: something I will see often, such as a sticky note on my mirror, my truth as the background picture on my phone, or a note on my car dashboard.
Pick one or two that fit me
  • Trust and support log: Each day, record one time someone showed kindness, kept a promise, listened, or offered help. Describe what I allowed myself to receive and what I learned.
  • My name in Scripture: Rewrite my verse with my name in it and read it aloud each morning. For example, a personalized adaptation of Psalm 56:3 (based on BSB) is: “When Sarah is afraid, she puts her trust in You.”
  • Gratitude journal: Each night, write three things I thank God for.
  • Truth recording: Record my own voice reading my Truth Card and verse, and listen in the car or on a walk.
  • Reading: The “Trust” topic in Pearls.
  • Listening: The Overcoming Anxiety audio by Dr. Brian Campbell. Tap to play.

Choose a modest, ordinary situation. These exercises do not require revealing private information or approaching anyone who has threatened, coerced, or abused you. A respectful response is useful evidence; a boundary violation is useful evidence too.

6

My action step this week

Do one thing that tests the lie in real life.

  • Accept one small offer of help. Choose a person who has shown reliability. Accept ordinary help, such as help with a task, and notice what actually happens.
  • Ask someone to listen. Tell a caring friend about a modest everyday concern and ask for a few minutes of listening. Compare their actual response with what I predicted.
  • Make room for companionship. Invite a dependable friend to a short walk, coffee, or another comfortable shared activity. Notice moments of warmth or connection.
  • Acknowledge dependable care. Thank someone for a specific way they have supported me. Write down how it feels to recognize and receive that care.
