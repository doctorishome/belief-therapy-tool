Psychological/Cognitive False Belief #4 · Personality Patterns · Distrust of People

“People are generally untrustworthy.”

✦ ✦ ✦

Personality Patterns · Mistrust and Suspicion
An educational example of expecting people, in general, to be dishonest, selfish, or likely to harm us. Having this thought does not mean a person has a personality disorder.

Where it often comes from

Betrayal, abuse, bullying, humiliation, or unstable relationships can teach a person to expect harm. Growing up where adults were unpredictable or lied can make caution feel like the only safe plan. A lesson learned from particular people can slowly become a rule about everyone.

How the belief can keep itself going

If we assume people cannot be trusted, we may keep our distance, test others, or watch for signs of betrayal. This can lead to loneliness and to misreading ordinary behavior as a threat. Staying apart also prevents us from collecting evidence about who is actually trustworthy. Careful, gradual trust can help us learn that some people are safe and some are not.

What Scripture teaches

Scripture is realistic about people. It warns against believing everything and calls us to be shrewd, and it also praises faithful friends. God, not any person, is our ultimate refuge, which frees us to trust people carefully rather than either blindly or never. These passages do not tell us to ignore real danger or to stay in unsafe relationships.

“The simple man believes every word, but the prudent man watches his steps.” (Proverbs 14:15)

Common self-statements

  • People will let me down if I give them a chance.
  • Everyone has a hidden agenda.
  • It is safer to count on no one.
  • If someone seems kind, they must want something.
  • People only look out for themselves.
  • I was right not to trust them, and I will be right again.

Common logical errors

  • Overgeneralization: Turning what a few people did into a rule about all people.
  • All-or-nothing thinking: Believing people are either perfectly safe or completely dangerous.
  • Fortune telling: Predicting betrayal before it happens.
  • Selective attention: Noticing every disappointment while overlooking faithful people.
  • Emotional reasoning: Taking the feeling of being on guard as proof that others are dangerous.
  • Discounting the positive: Dismissing acts of kindness or honesty as exceptions.

Challenging questions

  • Who, specifically, has hurt me, and what makes me sure everyone else will do the same?
  • Can I think of anyone who has been honest, loyal, or kind over time?
  • What does the evidence show about how this person treats others and keeps small promises?
  • Is there a difference between “not everyone is safe” and “no one is safe”?
  • How much trust is wise to give this person right now, for this particular matter?
  • What did I learn that is true from being hurt, and what did I turn into a rule that goes too far?
  • How might trusting God as my refuge help me trust people more wisely?

Truth summary

Some people are not trustworthy, and some are. I can learn from painful experiences without deciding that everyone will hurt me. Trust can be given in small steps and adjusted as I see how a person actually behaves. God is my refuge, so I do not have to guard myself from everyone to be safe.

A short prayer

Lord, You are trustworthy when people have not been. Heal the places where I was hurt. Give me wisdom to see who is safe, courage to take small steps toward connection, and peace that I am held by You. Amen.

Clinical background

For counselors: beliefs like this are often discussed in connection with what clinicians call paranoid personality disorder. This card follows Identify → Challenge → Replace, using evidence review, balanced alternatives, daily rehearsal, and small behavioral experiments. Generalized distrust can follow betrayal, trauma, or abuse and may occur with anxiety, depression, or other conditions; it is not specific to a personality disorder. This is an individualized educational formulation, not a DSM criterion or a diagnostic test. Psychological difficulties have multiple contributing factors. Distrust can be reasonable when someone is unsafe; experiments should be low-stakes, and anyone in an abusive or dangerous situation should seek safety and professional help.

Try a small behavioral experiment

Choose a safe, low-risk test; do not ignore real warning signs.

  • Make a short list of people who have been honest or reliable, even in small ways. Add one new example each day this week.
  • Choose a person who has kept small promises. Share a small, low-risk detail and notice how they treat it.
  • Ask a reliable person for a small favor. Compare what you predicted with what actually happened.
  • Notice three everyday kindnesses from others today without looking for a hidden motive. Write what you saw.
  • Keep a “trust log”: when someone does what they said, record it. Review it after a week.
  • Write an honest prayer about your fear of being let down, then consider one small step toward someone safe.
