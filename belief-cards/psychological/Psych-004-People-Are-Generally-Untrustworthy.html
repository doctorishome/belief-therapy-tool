Psychological/Cognitive False Belief #4 · Personality Patterns · Hidden Intentions

“People always try to hide what they are doing”

✦ ✦ ✦

Believing you have been deceived, betrayed, or violated by someone’s harmful actions can lead you to suspect that they have hidden plans. Family secrecy, unpredictable behavior, or repeated warnings about other people can strengthen the belief that you must always search beneath the surface to find hidden meaning or clues to planned behavior. Even ordinary privacy or an incomplete explanation may then feel like evidence of deception.

Where it often comes from

Being deceived, betrayed, or surprised by someone’s harmful actions can teach you to expect hidden plans. Family secrecy, unpredictable behavior, or repeated warnings about other people can strengthen the rule that you must always search beneath the surface. Even ordinary privacy or an incomplete explanation may then feel like evidence of deception.

How the belief can keep itself going

Expecting concealed intentions can lead to checking, repeatedly questioning people, or replaying conversations for clues. Checking may briefly ease anxiety, but it can make uncertainty feel harder to tolerate. Another person’s discomfort with repeated questioning may then seem to confirm the original suspicion. Pausing, asking one respectful question, and comparing predictions with observable behavior can interrupt this cycle.

What Scripture teaches

God invites us to bring our worries to Him and receive His peace. We are not required to uncover every private thought or predict every action. Love makes room for patience and hope, and faithful friendship can grow through experience. Trusting God helps us respond to what is actually happening instead of treating every unanswered question as proof of deception.

Common self-statements

  • They are keeping something from me.
  • If I stop watching, they will catch me off guard.
  • There must be another reason they are doing this.
  • I need to work out what they are really planning.
  • A vague answer means they are deceiving me.
  • I cannot relax until I know their true intentions.

Common logical errors

  • Mind reading: Assuming I know an intention that has not been stated.
  • Overgeneralization: Turning past deception into an expectation about everyone.
  • Jumping to conclusions: Treating incomplete information as proof of a hidden plan.
  • Confirmation bias: Looking for clues that support suspicion while dismissing openness.
  • Catastrophizing: Predicting harm whenever I do not know someone’s plans.
  • Emotional reasoning: Treating anxiety as evidence that deception is occurring.

Challenging questions

  • What did I directly observe, and what intention am I guessing?
  • Does missing information necessarily mean deliberate deception?
  • Could privacy, distraction, uncertainty, or a change of plans explain this?
  • What consistent actions support a more trusting interpretation?
  • Can I ask one clear question without accusing or repeatedly checking?
  • What would I do differently if I could tolerate not knowing everything?

Truth summary

I cannot know every thought or plan another person has. Uncertainty and ordinary privacy do not prove deception. I can ask clear questions, notice consistent actions, and allow reliable people to earn my trust. I can place my anxiety in God’s care without constantly searching for hidden intentions.

A short prayer

Father, help me separate what I see from what I fear. Calm the urge to search for hidden motives. Give me patience to listen, courage to ask kindly, and peace to leave unanswered questions with You. Help me recognize and receive honest care. Amen.

Clinical background

For counselors: beliefs like this are often discussed in connection with what clinicians call paranoid personality disorder. This card follows Identify → Challenge → Replace, using evidence review, balanced alternatives, daily rehearsal, and small behavioral experiments. Generalized distrust can follow betrayal, trauma, or abuse and may occur with anxiety, depression, or other conditions; it is not specific to a personality disorder. This is an individualized educational formulation, not a DSM criterion or a diagnostic test. Psychological difficulties have multiple contributing factors. Distrust can be reasonable when someone is unsafe; experiments should be low-stakes, and anyone in an abusive or dangerous situation should seek safety and professional help.

Try a small behavioral experiment

Choose a safe, low-risk test; do not ignore real warning signs.

  • In an ordinary interaction with a reliable person, write the observable facts separately from your guess about their intentions. List two other explanations.
  • Ask one neutral question about a small uncertainty, such as “Could you explain the plan?” Listen without repeating the question for reassurance.
  • When there is no concrete sign of danger, postpone an unnecessary check for 15 minutes. Rate your anxiety before and after, then return to an ordinary task.
  • Agree on one small shared plan with a dependable friend. Compare what you predicted with what the person actually did.

Choose an ordinary, low-stakes situation. Do not use these exercises with someone who is threatening, coercive, or abusive, or to dismiss concrete evidence of deception.
