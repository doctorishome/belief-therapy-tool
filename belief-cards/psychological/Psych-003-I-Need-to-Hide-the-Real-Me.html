Psychological/Cognitive False Belief #3 · Personality Patterns · Hiding the Real Me

“I need to hide the real me in order to not get hurt.”

✦ ✦ ✦

Deep down, do you fear that others, if they got to know you, would hurt, ridicule, or make fun of you? Because of this, do you hide your real feelings or the real you? The information below is intended to help you lessen your fear of others by trusting in God’s love and care for you as expressed in the Bible. You don’t need to hide from God and, with God’s help, you can start to be more open and trusting with other people.

Where it often comes from

If honesty was met with ridicule, rejection, punishment, or betrayal, hiding may once have felt protective. A person may learn to show only a polished version of themselves. Over time, a response to particular experiences can become a rule: being known always leads to being hurt.

How the belief can keep itself going

Hiding feelings, agreeing when we disagree, and pretending to be fine can reduce anxiety briefly. But others have fewer opportunities to understand or support us. Feeling unseen can then seem to prove that closeness is impossible. Small, chosen moments of honesty can help us learn whether a trustworthy person responds with care.

What Scripture teaches

God knows us deeply and invites us to approach Him with confidence. His acceptance gives us a foundation for living more honestly. Scripture also calls believers to welcome one another and share burdens. These passages invite connection; they do not promise that every person will respond well or require us to share everything with everyone.

“O LORD, You have searched me and known me. You know when I sit and when I rise; You understand my thoughts from afar.” (Psalm 139:1–2)

Common self-statements

  • If people really knew me, they would use it against me.
  • I must look strong and keep my struggles hidden.
  • My honest feelings will drive people away.
  • It is safer to be what others expect.
  • I should say I am fine even when I need support.
  • Being known means losing control.

Common logical errors

  • Overgeneralization: Turning past rejection into a rule about all relationships.
  • Fortune telling: Treating a feared hurt as certain before it happens.
  • Mind reading: Assuming people would reject the real me without checking.
  • All-or-nothing thinking: Believing I must either hide everything or reveal everything.
  • Emotional reasoning: Taking the feeling of vulnerability as proof that I am unsafe.
  • Discounting the positive: Dismissing experiences of being accepted when I was honest.

Challenging questions

  • What part of myself am I afraid to show, and what do I predict will happen?
  • Am I assuming this person will react like someone who hurt me before?
  • Who has already responded with kindness when I was honest?
  • Does keeping everything hidden protect me, or also leave me lonely?
  • Can I share one small, ordinary truth instead of everything at once?
  • Could someone disagree with me and still care about me?
  • How might God’s knowledge and acceptance of me change this rule?

Truth summary

I do not have to hide all of myself to be worthy of love or to build meaningful relationships. God knows me and welcomes me. I can allow people who have shown care and respect to know me gradually. Honesty cannot guarantee that I will never be hurt, but hiding everything also has a cost. I can be authentic and still choose healthy boundaries.

A short prayer

Lord, thank You for knowing me and inviting me close. Help me release the need to appear perfect. Give me courage to be honest, wisdom about whom to trust, and openness to receiving love. Amen.

Clinical background

For counselors: beliefs like this are often discussed in connection with what clinicians call paranoid personality disorder. This card follows Identify → Challenge → Replace, using evidence review, balanced alternatives, daily rehearsal, and small behavioral experiments. Hiding the self can occur with suspiciousness, shame, social anxiety, or past relational injury; it is not specific to a personality disorder. This is an individualized educational formulation, not a DSM criterion or a diagnostic test. Psychological difficulties have multiple contributing factors. Sharing personal information is a choice; experiments should involve ordinary, low-stakes honesty with someone who has shown respect.

Try a small behavioral experiment

Choose a safe, low-risk test; do not ignore real warning signs.

  • With a person who has respected you, express a small preference instead of automatically agreeing. Predict their response, then record what they actually do.
  • When a trusted friend asks how you are, share one ordinary feeling, such as being tired or a little worried. Notice whether they listen with care.
  • Ask a reliable person for a small favor. Compare what you predicted with the response you received.
  • Admit a minor, everyday mistake to a kind person. Notice whether being imperfect prevents connection.
  • Tell a trusted person about a harmless interest or opinion you usually keep hidden. Look for evidence of curiosity, acceptance, or respectful disagreement.
  • Write an honest prayer about a feeling you hide. Then consider one small part you might choose to share with a trustworthy person.
