Psychological/Cognitive False Belief #22 · Closeness and Steadiness · Being Truly Known

“If people really knew me, they would not accept me.”

✦ ✦ ✦

Do you feel you have to hide who you really are, or that you will be rejected if people see the real you? Do you sometimes wonder who you are underneath? The information below is intended to help you take a step toward being known and accepted, and to find the security that comes from knowing God’s word as found in the Bible.

Where it often comes from

This belief often grows where love felt conditional: where mistakes brought anger or silence, where a person had to earn approval, or where feelings were dismissed. A child in that setting learns that the real self is a problem and a performed self is safer. Over time, the person may feel unsure who they are beneath the roles they play.

How the belief can keep itself going

If I hide the real me, closeness stays shallow, and shallow closeness never tests the fear. Acceptance of my mask does not feel like acceptance of me. Or I may reveal too much too fast, then panic when someone reacts, which seems to prove the belief.

What Scripture teaches

God already knows everything about us. “O LORD, You have searched me and known me.” And He loves us anyway: “God proves His love for us in this: While we were still sinners, Christ died for us.” Because of that, we are told, “Accept one another, then, just as Christ accepted you.” Being fully known and fully loved is where the believer stands. Healthy closeness grows step by step with people who have shown they are safe.

“Accept one another, then, just as Christ accepted you, in order to bring glory to God.” (Romans 15:7)

Common self-statements

  • If they knew the real me, they would leave.
  • I have to be careful what I show.
  • I do not even know who I am.
  • Something is wrong with me.
  • I am too much, or not enough.
  • I am only liked when I perform.

Common logical errors

  • Mind reading: Assuming I know how people would react to the real me.
  • Fortune telling: Predicting rejection before I have been known.
  • Emotional reasoning: Feeling unacceptable and concluding I am.
  • All-or-nothing thinking: Believing I am either perfectly acceptable or totally unacceptable.
  • Labeling: Calling myself defective instead of describing a flaw.
  • Discounting the positive: Dismissing acceptance as being only of my mask.

Challenging questions

  • What exactly do I believe people would reject?
  • Has anyone known something real about me and stayed?
  • Where did I learn that the real me was a problem?
  • Does God’s full knowledge of me change what is true?
  • Am I sure how people would react, or am I guessing?
  • What would it look like to share a little more, with a safe person?
  • If a friend felt this way, what would I tell them?

Truth summary

God knows everything about me and loves me. I am imperfect but deeply loved, and Christ has accepted me. I do not have to hide to be worthy of love. I can be known gradually by safe people and let go of the mask.

A short prayer

Lord, You know me completely and love me still. Help me to rest in Your acceptance, to be honest about who I am, and to find safe people with whom to be known. Amen.

Clinical background

For counselors: this belief appears in the cognitive model of what clinicians call borderline personality disorder (Beck, Davis, & Freeman, Cognitive Therapy of Personality Disorders), where fear of abandonment, all-or-nothing thinking about people, an unsettled sense of self, and a fear of being overwhelmed by emotion are central. An unstable or poorly defined sense of self is a recognized feature; the work is gradual: build identity in Christ, practice honest self-disclosure in graded steps, and watch for oversharing followed by panic. Behavioral skills for tolerating distress and regulating emotion (as in Linehan’s DBT) pair well with this card’s Identify → Challenge → Replace work. Screen for self-harm and suicide risk, and consult or refer when risk is present (in the U.S., 988). This is an individualized educational formulation, not a DSM criterion or a diagnostic test. Similar presentations can arise from trauma, depression, anxiety, or temperament, so careful assessment matters. Pace any experiments to the person.
