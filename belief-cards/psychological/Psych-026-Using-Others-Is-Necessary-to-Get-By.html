Psychological/Cognitive False Belief #26 · Honesty and Respect · Getting By

“Using other people is necessary to get by.”

✦ ✦ ✦

Have you learned that you have to look out for yourself, because nobody else will? Do you sometimes see people as either useful or in the way? The information below is intended to help you take a step toward a more secure way to live, and to find the provision that comes from knowing God’s word as found in the Bible.

Where it often comes from

People who grew up where needs went unmet, where adults could not be trusted, or where only the strong did well often conclude that survival means getting what you can from others before they get it from you. For a time this view may have even protected you. It makes sense of a hard past, though it can cost you the trust and loyalty you actually need.

How the belief can keep itself going

If I treat people as tools, they eventually pull away, distrust me, or treat me the same way, and that seems to prove the world is as hard as I thought. Short-term gains hide the long-term loss of real friends, work, and peace of mind.

What Scripture teaches

Scripture shows a different way to be provided for. “And my God will supply all your needs according to His glorious riches in Christ Jesus.” “Seek first the kingdom of God and His righteousness, and all these things will be added unto you.” Those who work and share live well: “A generous soul will prosper, and he who refreshes others will himself be refreshed.” And the Bible calls us to change course: “He who has been stealing must steal no longer, but must work, doing good with his own hands, that he may have something to share with the one in need.”

Common self-statements

  • Nobody looks out for me but me.
  • Everyone is out for themselves.
  • If I do not use them, they will use me.
  • People are there to be used.
  • Only suckers play fair.
  • I do what I have to do.

Common logical errors

  • Overgeneralizing: Concluding that everyone is out to use me because some people were.
  • All-or-nothing thinking: Seeing people as either useful or a threat.
  • Fortune telling: Predicting that if I do not take first, I will lose.
  • Discounting the positive: Overlooking people who have helped me without wanting anything.
  • Short-term thinking: Counting what I gain now but not what it costs later.
  • Emotional reasoning: Feeling that the world is harsh and concluding that treating people harshly is the only way.

Challenging questions

  • What happened that taught me nobody would look out for me?
  • Has using people ever cost me a relationship or an opportunity?
  • Who in my life has helped me with nothing in return?
  • What do I really need that I am trying to get this way?
  • Could there be a safer way to get what I need?
  • What does Scripture say about how God provides?
  • If my child or friend lived this way, what would I hope for them?

Truth summary

I can meet my needs without using people. God supplies what I need, and work, honesty, and fair dealing build lasting trust. I can leave behind the old way of surviving and build a life where I am secure and respected.

A short prayer

Lord, You know the hard road that taught me to look out only for myself. Provide what I need, heal my distrust, and teach me to live honestly and fairly with others. Amen.

Clinical background

For counselors: this belief appears in the cognitive model of what clinicians call antisocial personality disorder (Beck, Davis, & Freeman, Cognitive Therapy of Personality Disorders), where views such as “I have to look out for myself,” “others are weak and exploitable,” and “rules do not apply to me” are central. Exploitation is often a learned survival strategy; explore early deprivation, abandonment, or mistreatment, and the short-term payoffs versus long-term costs. Expect little spontaneous distress about these beliefs; engagement usually begins with the person’s own goals and costs (relationships, legal trouble, loss of trust), so use collaborative questioning rather than confrontation, set clear boundaries, and keep sessions practical. Assess risk of harm to others, abuse or violence in the home, substance use, and suicide risk; consult or refer as indicated (in the U.S., 988 for crisis). This is an individualized educational formulation, not a DSM criterion or a diagnostic test. Similar presentations can arise from trauma, a hard upbringing, substance use, or other conditions, so careful assessment matters.
