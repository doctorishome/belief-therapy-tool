Psychological/Cognitive False Belief #15 · Personality Patterns · Not Worth the Effort

“Relationships take more than they give, so they aren’t worth the effort.”

✦ ✦ ✦

Personality Patterns · Avoidance of Others
An educational example of weighing relationships like a ledger, concluding that the cost is always greater than the benefit. Having this thought does not mean a person has a personality disorder.

Where it often comes from

Someone who grew up caring for others, or in a home where closeness meant constant demands and little warmth, may learn that people take far more than they give. If the benefits of friendship were rarely experienced, the cost is all that is remembered.

How the belief can keep itself going

If the person invests little, relationships give little, and that seems to prove the belief. Time with draining people is counted, while unseen rewards are missed because they have never been tried. Looking at the whole picture, including what has been lost by staying away, can change the balance.

What Scripture teaches

Scripture does not hide the cost of love, but it tells us that giving is where blessing is found. Jesus said it is more blessed to give than to receive, and a generous person is refreshed. We are called to encourage one another and not neglect meeting together. These passages do not ignore draining relationships; they point to the fruit of well-chosen, mutual ones.

“And let us consider how to spur one another on to love and good deeds. Let us not neglect meeting together, as some have made a habit, but let us encourage one another, and all the more as you see the Day approaching.” (Hebrews 10:24–25)

Common self-statements

  • People are exhausting.
  • I give and give and get nothing back.
  • It is not worth the energy.
  • I have better things to do than keep up friendships.
  • Relationships are more work than they are worth.
  • I am better off with fewer demands.

Common logical errors

  • Mental filter: Focusing on the costs and overlooking the benefits.
  • Overgeneralizing: Concluding that all relationships are draining because some were.
  • All-or-nothing thinking: Seeing relationships as either fully rewarding or a waste.
  • Discounting the positive: Dismissing good moments as exceptions.
  • Emotional reasoning: Feeling tired and concluding that people are not worth it.
  • Fortune telling: Predicting that new relationships will cost more than they give.

Challenging questions

  • What exactly do I think relationships cost, and what might they give?
  • Which relationships, past or present, have been good for me?
  • Have I ever invested in a relationship that was mutual and healthy?
  • Is the problem relationships in general, or specific draining ones?
  • What has staying away from people cost me?
  • What does Scripture say about the blessing of giving and encouraging?
  • How could I set limits so that relationships cost less but still give something?

Truth summary

Some relationships are draining, and I am allowed to set limits. But healthy, mutual relationships give comfort, laughter, help, and growth, and I may not have experienced much of that yet. I can choose wisely, give a little, and watch what comes back. God made relationships a source of blessing, not only of cost.

A short prayer

Lord, You know how tiring people have been for me. Heal what has been draining, and teach me healthy limits. Show me one relationship worth investing in, and let me discover the blessing You placed in it. Amen.

Clinical background

For counselors: beliefs like this are often discussed in connection with what clinicians call schizoid personality disorder, a long-standing pattern of detachment from close relationships and a narrow range of expressed emotion. This card follows Identify → Challenge → Replace, using evidence review, balanced alternatives, daily rehearsal, and small behavioral experiments. A cost-benefit view of relationships can reflect real past depletion; exploring what the person has actually received, and teaching limit-setting, avoids dismissing legitimate experiences. This is an individualized educational formulation, not a DSM criterion or a diagnostic test. Similar presentations can arise from depression, trauma, social anxiety, autism spectrum differences, or temperament, so careful assessment matters. Pace any experiments to the person; someone with severe depression or thoughts of self-harm should seek professional help.

Try a small behavioral experiment

Choose a safe, low-risk test; do not ignore real warning signs.

  • Make two lists about one relationship: what it costs and what it gives. Compare them honestly.
  • Choose one person who has been good to you and spend a short, planned time with them.
  • Do one small kind act for someone and notice how you feel afterward.
  • Set one limit with a draining person, such as ending a call after twenty minutes.
  • Attend one group or gathering for a short, set time, and note what you gained.
  • Write a prayer thanking God for one person who has been a gift, and tell that person thank you.
