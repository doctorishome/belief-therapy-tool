Psychological/Cognitive False Belief #23 · Closeness and Steadiness · Standing on My Own

“I can’t manage life without someone to lean on.”

✦ ✦ ✦

Do you feel lost or panicky when you are on your own, as if you could not manage without a particular person? The information below is intended to help you take a step toward quiet confidence, and to find the strength that comes from knowing God’s word as found in the Bible.

Where it often comes from

Some people never had the chance to learn they could cope. A parent who did everything, or a parent who was unreliable and left them anxious, can leave a person doubting their own ability. Later, one person comes to feel like the only source of steadiness, so the thought of losing them feels like losing the ability to live.

How the belief can keep itself going

Leaning heavily on one person leaves less practice at handling things alone, and less practice feeds the doubt. I may also stay in unhealthy situations or avoid disagreement to keep the person close. Each time I get through a hard moment, I may credit the other person instead of myself.

What Scripture teaches

God made us for one another, and leaning on others is healthy: “Two are better than one.” But our deepest support is God Himself: “God is our refuge and strength, an ever-present help in times of trouble.” Even when we feel weak, “My flesh and my heart may fail, but God is the strength of my heart and my portion forever.” We can say with Paul, “I can do all things through Christ who gives me strength.” The goal is not to lean on no one, but to rest on God first and to share life with others freely.

“God is our refuge and strength, an ever-present help in times of trouble.” (Psalm 46:1)

Common self-statements

  • I cannot do this on my own.
  • I would fall apart without them.
  • I need someone with me.
  • I am helpless alone.
  • I do not know how to handle things.
  • I cannot be left on my own.

Common logical errors

  • Catastrophizing: Imagining that being alone would be unbearable.
  • Fortune telling: Predicting that I would not cope.
  • All-or-nothing thinking: Believing I am either supported or helpless.
  • Discounting the positive: Overlooking the times I have handled things myself.
  • Emotional reasoning: Feeling panicky and concluding I cannot cope.
  • Labeling: Calling myself helpless instead of describing a struggle.

Challenging questions

  • What have I handled on my own before?
  • What exactly do I think would happen if this person were away?
  • Is it true I have no skills, or have I just had little practice?
  • How has God helped me in past hard times?
  • What is one small thing I could do for myself this week?
  • Is it healthy to lean on others while also standing on my own?
  • What would I tell a friend who felt this way?

Truth summary

I can share life with others, and I can also stand. God is my refuge and strength, and I have handled hard things before. Being alone for a time does not mean being helpless. I can grow in confidence one small step at a time.

A short prayer

Lord, You are my refuge and strength. Help me to rest in You first, to grow in confidence, and to share life with others without losing myself. Amen.

Clinical background

For counselors: this belief appears in the cognitive model of what clinicians call borderline personality disorder (Beck, Davis, & Freeman, Cognitive Therapy of Personality Disorders), where fear of abandonment, all-or-nothing thinking about people, an unsettled sense of self, and a fear of being overwhelmed by emotion are central. Dependence on a single attachment figure and fear of being unable to cope alone are common; build self-efficacy through graded independence tasks, and distinguish healthy interdependence from avoidance of one’s own competence. Behavioral skills for tolerating distress and regulating emotion (as in Linehan’s DBT) pair well with this card’s Identify → Challenge → Replace work. Screen for self-harm and suicide risk, and consult or refer when risk is present (in the U.S., 988). This is an individualized educational formulation, not a DSM criterion or a diagnostic test. Similar presentations can arise from trauma, depression, anxiety, or temperament, so careful assessment matters. Pace any experiments to the person.
